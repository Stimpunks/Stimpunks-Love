Trees, crops and animals in the same fields

All Together Now Farms

Nut trees with crops between them, the Three Sisters, sheep, goats and chickens, and everything the farm grows going to the café in its old dairy and the honesty box on the street.

The sun is going down on one side of the farm and the full moon is coming up on the other, both on the horizon at once, so everything here is lit by two lights: warm on its west side and silver on its east. The barn has a warm wall and a silver one, every tree a warm edge and a cool one, and the sheep under the trees are the same. Nothing on this farm grows on its own. The trees shelter the crops, the animals graze under the trees, the chickens follow the animals, and what comes out of one goes into another.

From the gate

In words: the sky is apricot on the left, where the sun sits half down on the horizon, and periwinkle on the right, where a full moon is just up. A line of trees runs along the back. A brook winds in from the left. In the middle stand a red barn and a small white dairy beside it, each with a warm edge on its left wall and a silver edge on its right. In front, two rows of round nut trees, with mounds between them where corn stands up out of squash leaves and a bean winds up the corn. Sheep graze under the trees on the left, two goats on the right, and hens peck between them. Every tree and animal has a warm rim on its left side and a cool one on its right.

Mixed farming

A farm where the crops and the animals are one farm rather than two businesses sharing a gate. The definition a group of agricultural researchers worked from:

MFS are defined as farming systems conducted by households or by enterprises where crop cultivation and livestock rearing together form integrated components of a single farming system.

E. González-García, J. L. Gourdine, G. Alexandre, H. Archimède and M. Vaarst, “The complex nature of mixed farming systems requires multidimensional actions supported by integrative research and development efforts”, Animal 6(5): 763–777 (2012).

Their paper was about small island farms in the Caribbean, where land is short and storms are not, and it ends on a sentence this farm runs on:

We argue that farmers and other relevant stakeholders should form part of the team from the very start.

E. González-García, J. L. Gourdine, G. Alexandre, H. Archimède and M. Vaarst, “The complex nature of mixed farming systems requires multidimensional actions supported by integrative research and development efforts”, Animal 6(5): 763–777 (2012).

Agroforestry

Agroforestry is the intentional combination of agriculture and forestry to create productive and sustainable land use practices.

“Agroforestry Practices”, the USDA National Agroforestry Center.

The USDA names five practices. Four of them are on this farm, and the fifth says why it is not:

  1. Windbreaks

    Linear plantings of trees and shrubs to enhance, protect, and benefit people, livestock, and soil and water conservation.

    A line of trees along the north and west edges, between the farm and the weather.

  2. Riparian forest buffers

    Natural or re-established streamside forests made up of tree, shrub, and grass plantings.

    Along the brook, where it runs down to the sanctuary's wetland.

  3. Alley cropping

    Agricultural crops grown simultaneously with long-term tree crops.

    Rows of walnut and hazel trees with the crops grown in the alleys between them.

  4. Silvopasture

    Combines trees with forage and livestock production.

    The sheep and the goats graze under the nut trees in the shade, and the chickens follow them.

  5. Forest farming

    Cultivation of high-value crops under the protection of a managed forest canopy.

    Not here. The farm has no woodland of its own: the wood belongs to the campgrounds, and nobody has drawn a map of it.

Every practice’s first line is the USDA National Agroforestry Center’s, word for word.

Silvopasture is the deliberate integration of trees and grazing livestock operations on the same land.

“Silvopasture”, the USDA National Agroforestry Center.

The Three Sisters

Corn, beans and squash, planted together. They are Indigenous agriculture, and the story of them belongs to the nations that tell it, so it is not told here. The Onondaga Nation publishes a book of its own about them, by Dehowähda·dih – Bradley Powless, and the Oneida Indian Nation says almost every Native nation has its own telling. Read them there.

What this farm does is plant them the way the Onondaga Nation’s book describes the Haudenosaunee planting them: together, but not all at once. Corn goes in first, on a mound of earth; the beans go in beside the corn once it is up, and climb it; the squash goes in around the mound, and its leaves keep the mound shaded and damp and the weeds down, while the beans feed the soil the corn grows in. On this farm the mounds stand in the alleys between the nut trees.

The animals

Sheep, goats and chickens. Some of them are raised for meat, and the farm says so on the honesty box’s board; none of the residents at the sanctuary next door ever is.

  • Sheep

    Graze under the nut trees and between the solar farm's rows, which keeps the grass down with nobody mowing.

    They give wool, milk, cheese and meat, to the café in the old dairy and the honesty box.

  • Goats

    Clear the brush along the windbreak and the brook, and eat things nobody else will.

    They give milk, cheese and meat, to the café in the old dairy and the honesty box.

  • Chickens

    Follow the sheep round the pasture a few days behind them, scratching through it and eating what they find.

    They give eggs, and meat, to the café in the old dairy and the honesty box.

Their manure goes to the cannabis farm’s beds and the chaos gardens, and the alfalfa the cannabis farm grows comes back to them.

What grows

  • Corn, beans and squash. Grown together, the Three Sisters, on mounds in the alleys between the nut trees.
  • Walnuts and hazelnuts. From the trees in the rows.
  • Sunflower and squash seeds. Saved for next year, and the rest roasted for the café.
  • Herbs. Dill and coriander along the alley ends, alongside the cannabis farm's basil.
  • Flowers. Along the field margins, for the bees the solar farm's flowers bring in.

The milk is pasteurised

The milk here is the goats’ and the sheep’s, and every drop of it is pasteurised in the old dairy before it is drunk, made into cheese or put in the honesty box. There is no raw milk on this farm, and the people who know why say it plainly:

Pasteurization is crucial for milk safety, killing harmful germs that can cause illness.

“Raw Milk”, U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, in the public domain.

While good practices on farms can reduce contamination, they cannot guarantee safety from harmful germs.

“Raw Milk”, U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, in the public domain.

And in Colorado it is the law for anything sold:

Retail sales of raw, unpasteurized milk shall not be allowed.

Colorado Revised Statutes 25-5.5-117.

Where it goes

  • The Dairy, the farm’s old dairy, is a café now, and everything edible on this land ends up on its menu.
  • The Honesty Box is a farm stand on the street, with the farm’s wool, meat, milk, eggs, cheese and produce, prices on a board and money in a box.
