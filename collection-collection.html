The Collection Collection

A cabinet of our people’s collections. The house brings the furniture; the light is theirs.

In the picture: the gallery from the doorway, dark, with the cabinets standing apart on the bare floor. Ryan’s pens and ink: a writing box open on a little table, with a daylight desk lamp on an arm, switched off, because there is nothing in it yet; Ryan’s perfume: a tall cupboard with its door ajar, with a strip light inside the door, switched off, because there is nothing in it yet; Norah and her husband’s leather swatches and stamping tools: a pegboard on the wall over a bench, with no lamp yet, because the lamp is Norah and her husband’s to choose; Chelsea’s house plants: a plant stand in three steps, with no lamp yet, because the lamp is Chelsea’s to choose; Helen’s books and rocks: a chest of shallow drawers with books along the top and one drawer pulled out, with no lamp yet, because the lamp is Helen’s to choose.

Some of us keep things. Pens, and the inks that go in them; perfume; the swatches and tools a trade is made of; plants on every sill; books, and rocks. This is a gallery of our people’s collections, one cabinet each, and every cabinet has a lamp of its own chosen for the thing it lights. There are no house lights. What you can see in here is exactly what somebody has decided to show you.

Why keep a collection

Nobody in here needs a reason, and nobody is asked for one. But the things we keep have a way of being more than things, and two of our glossary entries say why better than a label could. A special interest is a whole way of paying attention, and a collection is often what one looks like from across a room:

Some special interests are short lived, and some last the lifetime of the person; but, however long they last, they are intense, delightful, and a vital part of autistic culture.

C.L. Lynch, 7 Cool Aspects of Autistic Culture, NeuroClastic, 2019. Read against NeuroClastic's own page on 2026-09-23. Special Interest, in our glossary.

And object personification: the pen that would be lonely in the drawer, the rock that is glad to be back on the shelf. That is not a mistake about pens and rocks. It is care, and it is ordinary, and in here it is how everything on display is treated.

Many of us identify with the items we love, and even feel a degree of empathy for them, as if they were alive.

Devon Price, Unmasking Autism, Harmony Books, 2022. Read off our own Object Personification entry, which quotes the book at pages 169 and 170; not checked against a copy of the book from here. Object Personification, in our glossary.

A cabinet with nothing in it yet stands with its lamp off, and its label is exactly as easy to read as the lit ones: an empty cabinet is not a lesser one, it is one that is waiting.

House rules

  • Nobody asks how many. A collection is not a number, and nothing in here is counted: not the pens, not the plants, not the cabinets.
  • Nothing is priced, valued or called rare. What a thing might fetch is none of our business, and a page that listed what people own with a price beside each thing would be a list for somebody else to shop from.
  • Nothing is ranked. No best cabinet, no biggest collection, and nobody’s pens set against anybody else’s.
  • Every cabinet has its own lamp, and no two lamps in here are alike. The owner chooses it. The house only ever guesses at the furniture.
  • A collection can be anything somebody keeps on purpose. It can stop for years, sit in a drawer, and start again, and nobody asks why.
  • Nobody explains their collection to anybody. “What is it for?” is not asked in here.
  • The pictures are of things, never of people. A photograph with a person in it belongs on the polaroid wall, which has its own promises and its own consent record.
  • Nothing in a picture says where you live. The location a phone writes into every photograph is stripped before a file comes anywhere near this site, and every frame is looked at for an address, a letter or a view out of a window before it goes up.
  • Nothing is filtered or cropped. A photograph is made smaller so the page loads, and that is all that is done to it. The lamp’s colour goes round the picture and never over it.
  • Everything comes down when you say so, one thing or the whole cabinet. It comes out of the repository too, and nobody asks whether you are sure.

Putting something in a cabinet

Send the photographs, and that is the whole job. Phone pictures are fine, badly lit is fine, the desk behind them is fine. You do not have to title anything, describe anything or caption anything: the house does the describing, and says it did.

That is the opposite of the polaroid wall, on purpose. The wall asks you to write the words for a picture of yourself, because a description of a person is theirs to write and nobody else’s. A description of a pen is not a description of anybody. It is work, and work is exactly the thing that stops people sending a picture at all, so in here the house takes it.

The describing is done by Claude, an AI, looking at each photograph, and every cabinet says so on its label. It writes only what it can see: colour, shape, what a thing is made of, what it is standing beside. It does not name a maker or a model from what a thing looks like, because a guess on a label reads as a fact. If a name is printed on the thing and can be read in the picture, it can go on the label; otherwise the name is yours to give, and yours wins over anything the house wrote.

Reach us through the Stimpunks contact page or in our community, say whose collection it is and how you want to be named, and it goes in a cabinet. To take anything down, the same routes, and the word remove is enough.

Whose these are

Every collection belongs to the person whose name is on its cabinet, and so does every photograph of it. The photographs are not under this site’s Creative Commons licence and carry no licence onward at all, so that taking one down stays possible: a Creative Commons licence cannot be revoked, and “it comes down when you say so” would otherwise be a promise the licence contradicted.

Every quotation belongs to whoever wrote it: C.L. Lynch at NeuroClastic, and Devon Price, whose book we have read only as far as our own glossary quotes it, and say so beside the line.

Every quotation in this room is 30 words or fewer, names who said it, where and when, and says beside it how it was checked. tools/make-collection.py refuses a longer one; refuses a photograph that still carries any metadata, that nobody has described, or that nobody has looked at for a person or an address; refuses a maker’s name in words Claude wrote unless it can be read in the picture; refuses a filter on any photograph; and refuses two cabinets with the same lamp.

Give me the answer

The answer is How many. A collection is not a number, and nothing in that gallery is counted — not the things, not the cabinets. A cabinet with a number beside it has become an inventory.

Taking the answer is a real way through this, not a lesser one. Nothing is watching and nothing is written down.
