Where every library is the same library

L-Space

Mass distorts space. Enough books distort it into something you can walk along.

A library is not just a building full of books. It is a place in L-space: every library, everywhere, joined to every other through the sheer weight of what is in them. Knowledge is power, power is energy, energy is matter, and matter is mass, which is Terry Pratchett’s arithmetic, from the Discworld books, and mass bends the space around it. Enough books bend it into something you can walk through. What you need to find your way is not a map. It is a ball of string.

A ball of string

That is how we think about a library. Not a catalogue. Not a curriculum. A space you move through by following a thread, finding what you need on the way to something else, and getting home again because you tied one end to the door. A monotropic mind walks L-space whether or not there is a library in it: one thread, followed all the way in, and the rest of the building bends round it. The name for that from the inside is a rabbit hole, and there is one of those on the street too.

Have you really read all these?

People ask it about a big shelf of books as if it were an accusation. They have misunderstood what a library is for. An unread book is not a failure. It is a resource, a future you got ready for, and the books you have not read yet are the most honest part of any collection: the part that says there is more to know. That is the anti-library. Neurodivergent people often build our libraries this way. We follow threads, we collect ahead of a hyperfocus that has not arrived yet, and we buy the book because something in us knew it before the moment to read it came. That is not disorganisation. It is monotropism working across time, and in L-space it is how the shelves get so heavy.

Books of the Edges

We call our reading lists Books of the Edges because of where we live. Disabled and neurodivergent people are always the edge cases. The middle of the world is built for some people, with its neat categories and its smooth timelines, and real life happens at the edges: in the breaks, the mismatches and the overlaps, where systems fail to account for how complicated people are and where new worlds come from. The name is from Octavia E. Butler, whose disabled narrator in Parable of the Sower calls her philosophy Earthseed: The Book of the Living, after the Books of the Dead. L-space is the one place where every edge of every library meets every other.
