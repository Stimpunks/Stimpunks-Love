pitch 02 · past the stream · left alone

Swaying Sweetgrass

A meadow, and almost nothing done to it. One path goes in, cut so that somebody using wheels can get to the middle, and at the middle there is a ring of stones with a fire in it and benches round the fire. That is the whole build. The rest is grass doing what grass does.

what is growing here

Left alone is a decision, not a gap in the plan

Every other pitch in this field is a building. This one is a field inside a field. Nothing here is landscaped, nothing is arranged for looking at from a particular spot, and there is no best time to come.

The argument for that is not that nature is nice. It is that a place you keep turning up to will tell you things, and will go on telling you for as long as you keep turning up — and that this is a relationship with obligations running both ways rather than a view with a bench in front of it. Our own glossary calls the learning half of this place-based: the natural, cultural and built environment as the text, and knowledge that belongs somewhere rather than everywhere. The other half is on Nature and on Offline — fresh air, daylight, large muscle movement, and the freedom to stim while you are out in it.

A correction we owe, kept where you can see it

Our glossary attributes a framework called reciprocal ethical unity to Barry Lopez. Star Stuff went and read the essay for The Place Knows You’re There and he never wrote the phrase. What he wrote was “living in some sort of ethical unity with a place”, and the hedge was doing work: he was being careful not to sound more certain than he was about somebody else’s relationship to the ground. Fusing that with a question he asks two paragraphs later turned a hesitation into a term of art. We did it, and this room is quoting from the pages where we did it, so it says so here rather than further down.

That zine also takes apart the sentence most people meet reciprocity through — as long as we protect nature, nature will protect us, which is Jessica Hernandez’s father’s teaching, reported by her. If you have spent your life being offered belonging on terms, you hear a contract in those first three words, and the flinch is correct and aimed at the wrong sentence. Nothing is being withheld. Walk far enough out of a relation and it does not start refusing you; it just cannot reach you.

And the attention this asks for is attention some of us get assessed for. The first thing Lopez names is paying intimate attention — one deep channel going a long way into one thing rather than a thin layer over everything. Read from where we are standing that is a description of monotropism. Same attention, different name, depending on who is doing it and whether anybody thought to ask what it was for.

the way in

One path, and it is everybody’s path

A meadow is not automatically anybody’s. Left completely alone it is a place for people who can wade through waist-high grass over uneven ground, which is a smaller group than it sounds and not the group this organisation is for. So there is a path, and the path has a specification, because “accessible” without one is a word in a brochure.

  • Firm, stable and still a natural surface. Compacted stone dust, not asphalt. You can get a chair or a rollator down it in the wet, and it still sounds like ground under you.
  • Wide enough to turn round on, and wide enough for two people to go in side by side, because arriving somewhere alone because the path was single file is its own kind of exclusion.
  • Somewhere to stop that is not the destination. There are places to sit along it, not only at the end. A route that only rewards you for finishing is a route designed by somebody who never needed to stop.
  • It says how long and how steep before you set off, the same way every press-to-play control on this street says how long it runs before you press it. Deciding needs the number in advance.
  • It is the only path. Not a step-free alternative round the back past the bins. One way in, and it is this one, and everybody uses it.

Past where the path stops, the grass is grass. We have not drawn a map of the rest of it, the way the field above has not drawn one of the woodland. That is on purpose too.

a door rather than a screen

The book read aloud, which is not ours to put on this page

Somebody has recorded most of Braiding Sweetgrass and put it on YouTube, chapter by chapter. It plays, it would embed, and it would have taken ten minutes to build it into this page as a row of screens beside the fire. Here is why it is a list of links instead.

The book is in copyright. Robin Wall Kimmerer is alive, publishing, and reads the authorised audiobook herself; the recording below is not hers and not her publisher’s. And the rule this entire room is built on is the honorable harvest — never take the first, never take more than you need, ask permission, give something back. A page making that argument out loud cannot serve somebody else’s whole book off its own surface while doing it. So the chapters are a door: press one and you leave, knowing what you are pressing.

They are worth knowing about, which is why they are here at all rather than quietly dropped. The gaps in the numbering are real gaps — those chapters were never uploaded — and they are left showing rather than closed up, because a tidy run of numbers would look like a whole book.

twenty-one strands

Seven behind, seven laws, seven ahead

A sweetgrass braid is made of twenty-one strands divided into three bundles of seven, and each bundle means something. This is not our teaching and we are not going to explain it; what follows is an Elder saying it, transcribed off the video it is said in, with the timestamp kept so you can go and hear it.

“we divide these 21 into seven seven and seven … we divide them into seven strands each and that's what we braid into the one braid”

  1. seven generations behind us

    the first seven they always represent those seven generations behind us — our parents, our grandparents, our great grandparents. Who we are, what we are, is because of them, because they're the ones that give us life. At least for that we need to be thankful.

Those teachings are powerful. Respect — powerful teaching. They say those teachings are really beautiful. Humility — when I can understand that I'm no better than anybody else, but then nobody else is any better than me.

“so we make that one braid, we bring them and make it into one braid, and in that way each of these strands they also represent yesterday today and tomorrow, man woman and child, mind body and spirit. You could take one strand, break it — that's simple. When you bring them all together, that's what creates that strength of that braid.”

“our whole journey, our whole life, is intertwined within this braid as well, and it begins right here with the roots that we don't see. Imagine a tree without any roots — a little bit of wind will come, that tree will fall down.”

the field guide

Sweetgrass, from the people it belongs to

Wiingaashk, wiingashk, swi’te’, Hierochloe odorata — the same plant, and the names are not interchangeable, because they come from different nations with different laws about it. Everything below is somebody else’s teaching on somebody else’s channel. We link it, we name who published it, and we say in one line of our own what it is about. We do not retell it, and there is nothing on this page that instructs anybody how to harvest, braid or burn sweetgrass, because that is not ours to hand out.

and this is not sweetgrass

The grass that is actually where you are

Ten seconds of a clump of bunchgrass gone to seed with the sun behind it, shot by Ryan in his own front yard on a January afternoon. It is not sweetgrass and we are not going to tell you what it is, because nobody here determined it and a plant named on a page reads as a determination.

It is here because it is the argument. Place-based does not mean going to the sacred meadow; it means the ground you are standing on, which for most people is a verge, a yard, a car park with something coming up through it. This clip is already on our own Nature entry, underneath the line about immediate contact with the outdoors, which is where it earned its place.

before you go

What this pitch does not do

It does not teach sweetgrass. It does not summarise an Elder. It does not tell you that being outdoors will fix anything, and it does not treat a meadow as a treatment. A loud room is an access barrier and so is a mile of uneven ground; this place is quiet because quiet is what it is for, and it has a path because a place with no path is closed.

The dial works here like everywhere. At Gentle the grass stands still and every word stays exactly where it is. Nothing is behind an intensity level.
