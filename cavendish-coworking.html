Cavendish Coworking

every door shut, and not one of them locked

Stimpunks is run in the open. Our team meetings are open to the world, because we default to open, and until now the only way into them was our Discord — which a great many people will not use, and that is their call to make rather than a thing to be talked out of. Open is not the same as an open-plan office. Nobody has to work in full view with every door propped, and nobody has to walk into a room to find out what is going on in it. So here the meetings are behind doors, the way a house has rooms: every one of them shut, every one saying on a note what is behind it and when, and none of them locked. The room our events meet in has a door here too. You do not need the Discord, an account, or anybody’s say-so to knock and go in.

Every note says when its room is in use before you open the door. The times are Central, as our events page gives them; where your browser will say, the note works out the same hour on your own clock underneath. Clocks do not all change for summer on the same day, so the week they change, trust the Central.

The house in Bedford Square

Two of Henry Cavendish’s houses were kept for opposite things. At Clapham Common, then outside London, he worked: a red-brick house whose drawing room he made into a laboratory, with a forge in the room next to it, an observatory upstairs and an eighty-foot ship’s mast in the grounds for a telescope. Nobody needed to be let in to see that something was going on there: the mast alone told the neighbours that the new resident was different. In Bedford Square he kept his library, and he opened it for borrowing, at all seasons of the year, with a librarian to look after it.

Anybody shown round it, his biographers write, would have concluded it was a house of knowledge. It was green throughout — green moreen curtains, green fire screens, green chair covers — and its mahogany blinds were lined with green transparent canvas: they could be down, and you could still see through them. It had rules, and the rules were his too. He signed the register for every book he took out of it to Clapham, like anybody else, and when the librarian died he gave a day a week to checking books out himself.

That is the house this room is built after. A place can be open without being open-plan. What makes it open is that anybody can find out what is behind each door and when, that the rules on the door apply to the people who run the house, and that nothing is locked. Our meetings are where the Stimpunks team does its work, and the team goes in by the same doors you do.

Everything on this page about Cavendish’s houses is from Christa Jungnickel and Russell McCormmach’s Cavendish: The Experimental Life, chapter 11, “Places” (second revised edition, 2016), read on 2026-09-25. The line about the librarian is their report of Thomas Young’s.

Why Cavendish

Cavendish Space is our name for psychologically and sensory safe space: caves for quiet reflection, campfires for learning together, watering holes for peer connection. We named it after Henry Cavendish, the eighteenth-century natural philosopher who discovered hydrogen, as Steve Silberman tells his life in NeuroTribes. Cavendish needed his cave. He also needed, now and then, a small set of his peers — the Royal Society’s Monday Club was his campfire — and he came to it on his own terms.

It is not true, however, that he wanted to remove himself entirely from the company of his peers; he just wanted to stand off to the side, soaking everything in.

Steve Silberman, NeuroTribes, p. 25, as our Cavendish Space entry quotes it

A door is how you get to do that. Our page on Cavendish Space quotes Annie Murphy Paul on why a place to work needs doors:

Perhaps the most important form of control over one’s space is authority over who comes in and out

Annie Murphy Paul, The Extended Mind, as our Cavendish Space page quotes it

Here that authority is yours, on the doorstep. You decide whether to knock, and once you are in, Proton will let you keep your microphone and camera off and stand off to the side, which is what the man the house is named after would have done.

On the other side of a door

Each door opens Proton Meet’s guest page for one room, in your browser. We opened every one of them on 2026-09-25 as far as that page and no further. Here is what was there:

  • No account, and nothing to install. It asks for a name, which can be whatever you would like to be called, and it names the room you are about to walk into, so you can check it is the right one. It offers a desktop app; you do not need it.
  • Your microphone and camera can stay off. If your browser has not been given them, it says so and offers to carry on without, and you can go in that way.
  • Proton’s own terms apply over there. Its page says that joining means agreeing to Proton’s terms and conditions and privacy policy. That is between you and Proton, and it is written here so that nobody finds it out after the knock.
  • The part of each link after the # is the room’s password, and it is there on purpose. These rooms are open, so it is not a secret; it is what takes you straight to the name box. A browser never sends that part of an address to any server, and this site tells Proton nothing on the way out but that you came from stimpunks.world.

The call is on the other side of the door and not in this page, and that was measured. Proton Meet will only let Proton’s own apps frame it, so a call inside this page would load as a blank box; and this street gives no page on it your camera or your microphone. Nothing on this page reaches Proton until you open a door.

What is not in here

  • No open plan. Nobody is on show, and nothing is propped open to prove it.
  • No lock and no list. Nothing to sign up for, and no Discord between you and the door.
  • No register of you. Cavendish kept one for his books; this page keeps nothing and sends nothing. The hour on your own clock is worked out in your browser, from its own clock and time zone, and goes nowhere.
  • No call inside the page. See above: it is behind the door, where calls go.
