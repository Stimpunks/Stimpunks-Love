Turning 07, and the only free market on the road

Nothing For Sale

A ring of cars in a lay-by with their headlights on, and everything laid out in the middle is free. Take what you need, leave what you can, and nobody is counting either.

The Outskirts is lit by your headlights. This turning is lit by everybody’s. Nobody who came out here brought enough light on their own, so everybody pulled into the lay-by in a ring, nose in, and left their lamps on, and the middle is as bright as a room. No two of those lamps match — an old estate on halogen, a newer van on LEDs, a hatchback running on one yellow fog lamp — so everything laid out in the middle throws a whole fan of shadows, one for every car, each with a slightly different colour at its edge.

Everything on the tables is free. It is the only free market on the road, and the only one where the word means that.

Solidarity, not charity

That phrase is the mutual aid movement’s rather than ours, and our Mutual Aid entry quotes it from a talk about Autistic mutual aid. The difference it names is the whole of this lay-by. Charity comes down, from somebody who has to somebody who has been judged to deserve it, and it wants to know you deserve it first. Mutual aid goes sideways. Sparrow, writing at Unstrange Mind, puts it like this:

Mutual aid is not charity. It is not a handout or an act of pity. It is a profound, ongoing, messy practice of loving each other into the future.

Sparrow, Mutual Aid: How We Survive, How We Transform, as our Mutual Aid entry quotes it

And our own Mutual Aid page ends its opening on the thing that actually helps:

showing up for each other, laterally, with no proof of worthiness required.

Two things of ours are not on a table, on purpose. Our mutual aid grants are not take-what-you-need: they have an application, limits on who can apply and a waiting list, and a lay-by that suggested otherwise would be promising something they cannot do. They are on our Mutual Aid page, which also says why they exist. And our Things Library argues for a library economy — tools, clothes, furniture, everything held in common — without yet listing anything to borrow, so there is nothing of it to lay out.

The rules of the lay-by

  • Nothing here is for sale, and nothing is a swap. Taking something does not put you in anybody’s debt, and nobody will ask for anything back. Out at Sithen there is a whole page about the old rule that eating what the fair folk offer leaves you theirs; this lay-by is that rule turned inside out, on purpose.
  • Nobody asks what it is for. No form, no proof, and no question about whether you need it enough.
  • Leave what you can, and nothing is a perfectly good amount.
  • Nothing is counted. Not what anybody took, not what anybody left, not who came.
  • Leave your headlights on if you have them. If you walked, there is light enough. That is what a ring is for.

Whose this all is

Every table is ours, and every quotation names who wrote it. The starter pack is David Gray-Hammond’s and Helen Edgar’s. The line about mutual aid not being charity is Sparrow’s, at Unstrange Mind. Solidarity, not charity belongs to the movement. Details are in the liner notes.

The lay-by is invented. Free shops, free tables and mutual aid networks are not, and they are mostly run by the people who need them.
