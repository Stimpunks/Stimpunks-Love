The Truck Stop

Truckin’ Food Court

Five trucks round a covered porch, each doing one thing several ways, and nothing with a price on it.

A lot of pale concrete in the sun, and a covered porch in the middle of it with picnic tables under the roof. Five trucks stand round it with their awnings out, and the sun comes through every awning in its stripes, so the colour at the Truck Stop is all under the awnings: laid across each counter and the ground in front of each hatch, a different two colours at every truck, and plain sun everywhere between. Everybody calls it the Truck Stop. It sits between the Run and the Green and serves both of them.

The trucks

Every truck does one thing, several ways, and leads with a vegan way of doing it, the same size as the rest. Every item says what is in it before you ask for it, whether it is vegan or vegetarian or which animal it came from, and which of the nine major food allergens the FDA names it has, and gluten, worked out from what is in it. Every drink says how much caffeine it has. Each truck is its own kitchen, so if an allergy is serious, say so at the hatch, and somebody will tell you what has been near what. Nothing is certified halal or kosher; every dish with meat in it says which animal.

Every truck has a low counter at the side of its hatch, no higher than 36 inches and at least 36 inches long, the 2010 ADA Standards’ figure for a service counter, and the tables on the porch are between 28 and 34 inches high, with room under them for a chair or a wheelchair.

Tacos

Every taco is on two corn tortillas warmed on the plancha, with lime and both salsas on the side: a red one of tomato and chile de árbol, and a green one of tomatillo, serrano chilli and coriander.

A taco is a Mexican dish: a small tortilla, corn or wheat, folded round a filling, in the words of Wikipedia's article on it.

Bánh mì

Every bánh mì is a Vietnamese baguette, thin in the crust and light in the middle, with đồ chua, the pickled carrot and daikon, and cucumber, coriander and fresh chilli in it as well as what it is named for.

The baguette came to Vietnam under French rule from the 1860s, and the sandwich became what it is in Saigon in the 1950s, Wikipedia's article on it says. The bread may be made with rice flour as well as wheat.

Ramen

Every bowl is wheat noodles made with kansui, the alkaline water that makes them springy and a little yellow, in a broth flavoured with its tare.

Wikipedia calls ramen a Japanese-Chinese fusion noodle dish, made with Chinese-style alkaline wheat noodles. Tare is the sauce that flavours the broth: shio, salt, is the oldest of the four kinds, and miso ramen comes from Sapporo.

    Contains wheat, soy, sesame and gluten.

  • Vegetable shio

    Vegan

    In it: ramen noodles of wheat, water and kansui, in a clear broth of kombu and vegetables with salt tare, with bok choy, bamboo shoots, nori and spring onion

    Contains wheat and gluten.

  • Spicy sesame

    Vegan

    In it: ramen noodles of wheat, water and kansui, in a broth of soy milk and kombu with sesame paste and chilli oil, with minced shiitake and bok choy

    Contains wheat, soy, sesame and gluten.

  • Shōyu

    Chicken

    In it: ramen noodles of wheat, water and kansui, in chicken broth with soy sauce tare, with roast chicken, bamboo shoots, nori and spring onion

    Contains wheat, soy and gluten.

  • Tonkotsu

    Pork

Treats

Everything on this truck is for a dog, and it says so on every bag. Take one across to the Run next door, where the dogs from the shelter are out, or to a dog of your own.

Nothing on this truck is on the ASPCA's list of people foods to keep from pets: no chocolate, coffee or caffeine, no xylitol, no grapes or raisins, no onion, garlic or chives, no milk, no nuts, no avocado, no citrus, no salt to speak of, no yeast dough or alcohol, and nothing raw. The tool that builds this board checks every bag against that list.

Nothing has a price

The trucks hand things out. There is no price on any board, no till, no jar and nothing to sign; take what you want, as many times as you want, and nobody asks who you are or why you are hungry. Our own entry on third places asks where you are when you are not at home or at work or school but at a place that provides community without being required to buy stuff, and this is one of the answers.

Next door

The Truck Stop is the second of the lots side by side where the buildings stop, and it serves both its neighbours. Through the gate at the left-hand end of the back fence is the Run, where the dogs from the shelter are out: its treat tin is filled from the treat truck here. Through the gate at the right-hand end is the Green, with a porch of its own to carry a cold drink to. Past the Green is the Pile.

House rules

  • Everything is free, and nobody keeps count of how many times you came back.
  • Sit anywhere on the porch, for as long as you like. Nobody clears a table that somebody is still at.
  • The treats are for dogs, and every bag says so.
  • Nobody at a hatch asks why you want what you asked for.
  • Nothing is counted: not the tacos, not the cups, and not you.

What the Truck Stop keeps

  • Nothing about you. What is on your table lives in this page and nowhere else, and is gone when you leave it. The Truck Stop’s own scripts store nothing in your browser and send nothing anywhere. A film on the screen loads from YouTube only when you press it.
  • Every card says what is in it before you ask for it, and every cup says its caffeine.
  • Nothing plays until you press play, and every film says how long it runs first.

Whose it is

The brief is Ryan Boren’s, of 2026-10-05, and so is the name, which borrows a word that has been doing this a long time: Robert Crumb’s cartoon Keep On Truckin’, in the first Zap Comix in 1968, and the Grateful Dead’s song Truckin’, on American Beauty in 1970, with words by Robert Hunter. None of the song is quoted here. Where each dish comes from was read on Wikipedia’s article on it, named on its truck’s board; the oral rehydration solution is the World Health Organization’s recipe; the allergen line is worked out by the same engine as Hey, Good Cookin’, the caffeine line with Pekoe and Purrs’ words, and every dog treat is checked against the ASPCA’s list, which the Run holds. The films on the rack are their channels’, and Ryan’s pick. The type is Sansita, by Omnibus-Type, and Familjen Grotesk, by Familjen STHLM AB, both under the SIL Open Font Licence. Every source is in the liner notes.
