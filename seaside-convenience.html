The Seaside Convenience

A public convenience on the front: painted iron, glazed tile, a glass roof, and the sea right there.

The convenience on the promenade: a long pavilion of glazed green tile under a glass canopy on curly iron brackets, a row of cream cubicle doors with a little lamp over each, an arch in the middle with an enamel sign that says Toilets, iron benches facing the sea, and the railings, the water and the glare beyond.

On the front

Go in at the shop door and you come out on a promenade, in the glare off the sea. Along the railings stands a convenience of the old seaside sort: a long pavilion of cast iron painted green and cream, its walls glazed tile, its roof a canopy of glass on curly brackets, so the whole inside is lit by the sky. It is a public toilet, and it is the nicest building on the front.

It is for everybody. There is nothing to pay at the turnstile, because there is no turnstile, and nobody at the door, because there is no need for anybody at the door. The sea is loud enough for both of you, and the building is quiet.

What it has

Every public restroom on the street has every one of these. Under each, how this one does it.

  • For everybody

    Every stall is for everybody, of every gender and none, and for anybody helping somebody else. No sign on the door says who may come in.

    There is one door off the promenade and one tiled hall behind it, and every cubicle opens off the hall. The enamel sign over the arch says Toilets, and that is all it says.

  • Toilets and urinals

    Toilets, and urinals too, each behind its own door.

    The toilets are in cubicles along the land side, and the urinals in cubicles of their own along the sea side, each behind a door like all the others.

  • Wheelchair accessible

    Step-free from the street, with a door that opens without a fight, room to turn a wheelchair right round, and grab bars beside and behind every toilet.

    A ramp comes down from the promenade at the gentlest slope the railings allow, beside the steps, and the doors open on a push plate and wait for you.

  • A step at the sink

    A Step ’n Wash under the basins: a step that folds out from under the sink for children, and for anybody else the basin is too high for, and folds itself away again.

    The basins are a long run of white porcelain under the window to the sea, and a Step ’n Wash folds out from under each one, so a small person can see the water while they wash.

  • Changing tables

    A changing table for a baby, and a height-adjustable one big enough for an adult, with a hoist, because a grown body needs changing too and almost nowhere has room for it.

    The end cubicle is the biggest, with a window over the water: the baby’s changing table folds down from its wall, and the adult one stands in the middle on its own pillar, with a hoist on a rail over it.

  • Menstrual products

    Pads and tampons, free, in every stall, so nobody has to ask or carry anything.

    In every cubicle there is a painted tin on the shelf with pads and tampons in it, filled every morning before the first walkers come along the front.

  • Condoms

    Condoms, free, in a bowl you can take from without being seen.

    A china bowl of condoms sits on the ledge by the door, where you can take a few on your way in or out and nobody can see which way you were going.

  • Sharps disposal

    A sharps box for needles, fixed to the wall in every stall, so nobody has to find somewhere else to put one.

    A sharps box is screwed to the tiles in every cubicle, at a height you can reach from the seat or from a chair.

  • Naloxone

    Naloxone, free, in a cabinet by the door with how to use it written on the inside, because it reverses an opioid overdose and somebody may need it before help comes.

    The naloxone is in a cabinet painted the same green as the ironwork, by the arch and never locked, with how to use it printed on the inside of its door in big letters.

  • Somewhere to sit

    Comfortable seats outside the stalls, for waiting, for whoever is helping somebody, and for a minute to yourself.

    Iron benches with wooden slats stand under the glass canopy, facing the sea, so whoever is waiting for somebody has the best seat on the front.

  • Soap and paper towels

    Soap, warm water and paper towels.

    Every basin has soap in a china dispenser, warm water from a tap you can turn with the side of a hand, and a tin of paper towels beside it.

  • No air dryers

    No air dryers. They are loud enough to hurt, and some of us cannot go in a room that has one.

    There is nothing on the walls that roars, so the loudest thing in the hall is the sea through the window.

  • Private stalls

    Every stall a small room of its own, its door down to the floor and up to the ceiling, with a light over it that says free or in use in words as well as colour.

    Every cubicle is a small tiled room of its own, its door down to the floor and up to the iron frame of the canopy, and over each door a little enamel lamp says Free in green or In use in red, in words big enough to read from the benches.

  • Shelves and hooks

    A shelf and a hook in every stall, for a bag, a coat, a cane or a phone, so nothing goes on the floor.

    There is a shelf in every cubicle for a bag, a hook up high and another low on the back of the door, and a peg by the basin for a bucket and spade.

  • Clear signs

    Signs in words and pictures, big, at a height you can read sitting down, with raised letters and braille.

    The signs are enamel on iron, cream letters on green, big enough to read from the railings, hung low enough to read from a chair, with the same words raised underneath and in braille.

  • Free, and nobody asks

    Free to use, at any hour, with nothing to buy first and nobody asking why you are there.

    It is free, it is open from before the first walker until after the last and all night in between, and nobody on the front will ask why you are going in.

Why a convenience should be this good

A building like this used to be a town’s way of showing off: the cast iron came out of a foundry catalogue, the tiles came out of a kiln by the trainload, and the whole thing said that a town that cared about you would build you somewhere good to go. Norway still builds its rest stops that way, along roads people drive to look at, and showed them off in October 2026 when it was called a name. This room is built in that spirit, on a front of our own.

Somebody needing a toilet is a fact about having a body. Every rule a restroom adds about who may use which door is a rule somebody gets caught on, and our Bathroom Access page names it.

What is not on the front

  • No sign says who may come in. The arch says Toilets, and every cubicle is for everybody.
  • Nothing to pay. No turnstile, no coin slot, nothing to buy at the kiosk first.
  • Nothing roars. No air dryers, and nothing else that shouts.
  • Nobody keeps watch. Nobody counts who comes in, how long anybody stays, or how often.
  • Nothing moves at any setting. The sea in the drawing lies still, and so do the gulls.
