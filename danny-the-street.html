the street is named after somebody

Danny the Street

Doom Patrol · DC Comics · 1990 · not ours

This site is a street with rooms on it that agree about nothing, and that was never our idea. It is Danny's. Danny the Street is a character in DC Comics’ Doom Patrol: a living street who turns up wherever somebody needs shelter, rearranges what is on them to suit whoever has arrived, and talks by changing the words written on their own surfaces. We borrowed the architecture and put a marquee across the top of the front page saying so. This page is where the name came from, what Danny has been since, and which parts are ours to take.

What Danny is

A living, sentient piece of urban geography, who can place themselves into any city’s layout without disturbing it — the buildings and the roads simply make room. Danny does it mostly at night, when nobody is looking, which means you can turn a corner on the way to work and find that you are walking down somebody.

What is on Danny is a joke with an argument inside it. The shopfronts are relentlessly masculine — a gun shop, an army and navy store, a hardware shop — and every one of them is hung with frills, lace, flowers and fairy lights. The street is in drag. That is the whole character in one image, and it is why a site about being unassimilable is laid out as a row of shops that do not match.

Danny has no voice and no face, so Danny speaks by rearrangement. Words appear in shop windows. A sentence turns up typed on a typewriter that happens to be there. Letters form out of the vapour coming off a manhole, or out of a scatter of broken glass. The English is thick with Polari, the slang carried through fairgrounds, the merchant navy, the theatre and gay Britain when being understood by the wrong person was dangerous — a way of speaking designed to be read only by people who could read it.

This is the part we stole most directly. On this street the page is the speech: there is no house style sitting above the rooms telling you what the site is, only the surfaces, each one saying a different thing in a different hand.

Where Danny came from

Danny first appeared in Doom Patrol (volume 2) #35, cover-dated August 1990, written by Grant Morrison and drawn by Richard Case. Morrison had taken the title over with #19 in 1989 and turned a superhero book about damaged people into a book about people the world has no slot for — which is the only kind of superhero book that was ever going to produce a street.

The name is a pun. Danny is named after Danny La Rue, the drag performer, whose stage surname is French for the street. Morrison has said as much in interviews since, and the joke tells you what the character is for: a female impersonator who is also a literal thoroughfare, so that the place and the person are the same thing and neither can be separated out for tidying.

The story Danny arrives in is a story about normalcy as the enemy. Danny and the people living there are hunted by Darren Jones and the Men from N.O.W.H.E.R.E., whose project is a world that behaves like a 1950s situation comedy. The Doom Patrol turn up to defend the street; what actually finishes Jones is the street’s own cabaret. A Disabled people’s organisation could not invent a better origin story if it tried: the antagonist is enforced ordinariness, and the defence is a floor show.

Flex Mentallo, who debuts in the same issue, is sheltered on Danny through the breakdown that follows his first failure. That is the other half of the character. Danny is not a base of operations. Danny is housing.

What Danny has been since

Danny is destroyed and reassembled more often than most characters get published, and every form keeps the same job.

  • 1990

    Danny the Street. Doom Patrol (vol. 2) #35, by Morrison and Case.

  • Later in that run

    Danny the World. Danny leaves this universe for an alternate Earth and becomes a whole one, pledged to shelter the people who need it across dimensions. Morrison’s last issue ends with a member of the team being brought home to live on Danny.

  • Volume 5

    Danny the Brick. Danny is levelled in Doom Patrol (vol. 5) #7. Crazy Jane gets out carrying one brick out of one of Danny’s buildings, with Danny’s sentience still in it, and carries Danny around in a pocket.

  • 2012

    Danny the Street again, briefly. Reintroduced in Teen Titans (vol. 4) #3, January 2012, in DC’s New 52 continuity, and later suggested to have survived as Danny the Alley.

  • 2016 on

    Brick, World, Dannyland, Ambulance. In Gerard Way’s Doom Patrol for DC’s Young Animal imprint, Danny goes from the brick back to a world, becomes an amusement park called Dannyland, manifests the comic-book character Casey Brinke into reality, and turns up as Danny the Ambulance.

A street, a world, a brick, an alley, a funfair and an ambulance. The thing that stays constant is not the shape; it is that whatever Danny is this month has a door in it.

Danny on television

Danny appears in the first three seasons of Doom Patrol (2019–2023), made for DC Universe and then HBO Max, as a fugitive from the Bureau of Normalcy — the show’s version of the Men from N.O.W.H.E.R.E., with the name saying the quiet part out loud.

The episode is “Danny Patrol”, season 1, episode 8, first shown on 5 April 2019, written by Tom Farrell and directed by Dermott Downs. This Danny is explicitly genderqueer and uses they/them pronouns, and the people who live on them are the Dannyzens — among them Maura Lee Karupt, a drag queen and a former Bureau agent who left the agency for the street it was hunting. Later seasons break Danny down to a brick and then rebuild them as an ambulance, which is the comics doing what the comics do.

There is one more screen version, and it is the odd one out: an episode of Teen Titans Go! called “Stickiest Situation”, in which Danny can talk out loud and move parts of themselves like limbs. It is the only Danny with a voice.

He, they, and which Danny is which

The comics write Danny as he, and describe the character in the vocabulary that was available in 1990 — a word for cross-dressing that has since aged into an insult in most of the mouths that use it. The television version is genderqueer and uses they/them. Both of those are facts about other people’s work and we are not here to adjudicate them.

Asked in 2020 why Danny had landed so hard with viewers, Morrison put it this way:

“Back in the day when he was created in the late 1980s, we didn’t have terms like ‘genderqueer’ or ‘non-binary,’ they just didn’t exist.”

Grant Morrison, interviewed by Kimberly Ricci, Uproxx, 11 August 2020

The Danny in our marquee uses they/them. That is a decision about this site rather than a correction of anybody’s canon: the street we borrowed is the one that shelters people whose category does not exist yet, and on a site run by and for neurodivergent and Disabled people, defaulting to they is what we would do for a person whose pronouns we had not been told. Danny has been a street, a world, a brick and an ambulance; they is the pronoun that survives all six.

What we took, and what we did not

We took the architecture. No single design system, on purpose: the rooms here share a base of ground rules and then refuse to look like each other, because the street rearranges itself for whoever walks down it. We took the rooms behind rooms, the field past the treeline for people who would rather not live on a street at all, and the empty shopfront with the keys in the door. We took the idea that a surface can be a way of speaking.

We did not take Danny. Danny the Street belongs to DC Comics, and nothing of theirs is on this page: no panels, no artwork, no dialogue. Every drawing here is ours and every one of them is a drawing of a road, which is a thing nobody owns. The one quotation above is an interview, quoted short and credited in full. What this page is, is a namesake credit — the same habit the liner notes keep for every song, typeface and borrowed phrase on the site, applied to the borrowed thing that is holding the whole building up.

And we did not take the vocabulary. 1990 was doing its best with the words it had. We have better ones and no excuse.
