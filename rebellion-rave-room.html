Turning 08, in the old hangar at the end of the airstrip road

Rebellion Rave Room

Peak Sensory Seek That Goes to Eleven

An aircraft hangar at the end of the airstrip road, taken over by the sensory seekers in our community who wanted somewhere to stim loudly and brightly. The ceiling is a very long way up, the walls are scaffolding, tier on tier of it, and the rig is bolted to every deck. This is the one place on the street where you will see a strobe: switched on by you, twice over, and off again the moment you say.

The ante-chamber

You come in through a small room first, with the hangar doors shut behind it. It is lit plainly and evenly, and everything you need to decide is on its walls before you open them.

Strobe and laser warning

The hangar’s rig has a strobe in it: flashes of bright white light, from one to eleven a second. Flashing light can cause seizures in people with photosensitive epilepsy, and it can bring on migraine, dizziness, nausea or disorientation in people without it.

Every rate the knob has is inside the range that can trigger a seizure. The Epilepsy Society says flashing between 3 and 30 times a second is the common range, varying from person to person, and 16 to 25 a second the most likely. It also says the UK’s Health and Safety Executive recommends that a strobe in a club or at a public performance flashes no more than four times a second. The knob starts at four. Everything above four is faster than that recommendation, and the knob says so as you turn it.

It is off, and it stays off until you switch it on and then say yes to a second question, which tells you the rate before you answer. Nothing on the rig starts by itself, and nothing is remembered: it is off again every time you come in.

Escape switches everything off at once, and so do the Stop buttons, going to another tab, and leaving the page.

The festival films on the big screen have their own lighting in them, strobe and lasers included. The rig cannot reach inside somebody else’s video, so none of its limits apply there.

House rules

  • You hold the rig. The lasers and the strobe are two separate switches, the rate is a knob, and you choose whether the rig stays in its picture or takes the whole screen. The dial does not switch any of it on or off: nothing on the rig runs until you ask, at any setting, and it stops when you ask, at any setting.
  • It goes to eleven, and it stops there. Eleven is a joke about amplifiers, and it is also where the knob ends. There is no twelve.
  • Seekers and avoiders share the floor. Nobody has to switch anything on, and nobody on this floor is too much. Leave the rig off and dance to the screen, or just sit at the edge.
  • Ask before you touch anybody. A dance floor is shared and a body is not.
  • You can leave whenever you like. The Cooldown Room is through the other door in here, and the rig does not reach it.
  • Nothing is counted. No number on the door, no streak of nights, and no board of who went to eleven.

The hydration station

Water, cold or at room temperature, in paper cups or into your own bottle, free and as much as you like. Beside it, a basket of foam earplugs, a hook of ear defenders, and a box of dark glasses for anybody who wants the dance floor without the rig in their eyes.

Through the doors: the hangar

The ceiling goes up and keeps going. It is all open floor, with tables and chairs round the edges and nothing in the middle but room. The DJ booth stands two floors up on its own tower at the back. Scaffold decks climb every wall, with the rig bolted to them: moving heads, speaker stacks and laser heads. The lift in the corner stops at every deck, so the stair beside the booth is not the only way up.

The light in here is light you can see in the air. The haze catches every beam along its whole length, so a laser is a line drawn across the room rather than a spot on a wall, and the strobe is light in time rather than in space: all of it, then none of it.

The rig

Two switches and a knob. Nothing here runs until you press it, and everything stops on Escape.

On the big screen

On the shared screen over the floor: three films that play on into YouTube’s own mix and one playlist somebody keeps. Nothing is fetched from YouTube until you press something. While the screen is dark, previous and next move along the channel list without loading anything at all. Once something is on, they change the picture, because by then you have asked for a screen.

Every film on this screen has a festival’s own lighting in it, flashing included, and the rig’s limits do not reach inside somebody else’s video. None of it is synced to the rig either: a page cannot see into another site’s player.

Whose this all is

Dance, Punks is out on the strip itself, and it is everything this is not: silent, steady, and lit only by headsets. It refuses a strobe and this room offers one, and both are right, because each is for the people who come to it. Go and dance there too.

Some of us stim with strobe lights, lasers, and flicker devices like the Lumenate Nova. We have no connection to Lumenate, and nothing on this page repeats what anybody says flicker does for a brain.

“Goes to eleven” is Nigel Tufnel’s amplifier, in This Is Spinal Tap (1984), directed by Rob Reiner and written by Reiner with Christopher Guest, Michael McKean and Harry Shearer, who played the band. Guest played Tufnel.

The flash rates in the warning are the Epilepsy Society’s, from its page on photosensitive epilepsy, read on 2026-09-27, and so is the Health and Safety Executive’s recommendation, which that page reports.

Tomorrowland, Thunderdome, Electric Love and Verknipt are their organisers’ names, and this hangar is not any of their events. None of these festivals, artists or channels is affiliated with Stimpunks, and none of them endorses us. Every channel on the screen, with links, is in the liner notes.

Give me the answer

The answer is Eleven. Eleven. It is a joke about Nigel Tufnel's amplifier in This Is Spinal Tap, and it is also where the knob really ends: the Epilepsy Society puts the flashing most likely to trigger a seizure at 16 to 25 a second, and the room's own tool refuses a knob that goes past eleven. It starts at four, the Health and Safety Executive's recommendation for a club.

Taking the answer is a real way through this, not a lesser one. Nothing is watching and nothing is written down.
