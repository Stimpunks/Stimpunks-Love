The Zibaldone

Somebody else already wrote it down.

A commonplace book, left open on the desk. People copy lines into it — from poems, from books, from each other — and beside each one they write what it is for. Every line in here says where it came from and how we checked it, because a quotation nobody can trace is just a nice-sounding sentence with a famous name stuck on the end.

The word

Zibaldone is Italian, and it means roughly a heap of things — the name given to the miscellany notebooks Italians have kept since about the fourteenth century, of which Leopardi’s enormous one is the famous example. Merchants kept them. So did poets. They are unruled, unsorted and unfinished on purpose: recipes next to accounts next to a line of verse somebody liked.

In English the same object is a commonplace book, which our own glossary has had an entry for since 2022. Montaigne kept one and more or less invented the essay out of it. Marcus Aurelius kept one and it turned into the Meditations. It is not a filing system. It is what you reach for when you cannot find your own words and somebody else’s will do.

This room exists because somebody in our community asked for it: a quote bank, quotes for any state of mind. That is what this is. The desk and the rules are ours; the good lines are everybody’s.

What this book will and will not do

A bank of quotations is the most dangerous shape of page on this street, and not for any reason you would notice from the outside. So the rules are in the generator rather than in somebody’s good intentions, and they are written here so you can hold us to them.

30 words

is the longest quotation this room will carry, and tools/make-zibaldone.py refuses a longer one rather than trusting anybody to remember. A bank of quotations is the shape of page that turns into republishing somebody’s book one reasonable-looking entry at a time. Where the line is not enough, the book is linked.

  • No song lyrics. Music publishing enforces on quotation where prose publishing shrugs, the licensing is separate from the recording, and one line is a far larger fraction of a song than of a novel. Nothing musical is hosted anywhere on this street, and a quote bank is exactly where that promise would have been broken in the way that looked most harmless. The tool refuses a song; it is not left to care. Bring us the person talking about the song instead — that we can quote.
  • Every line names its printing, not just its title. These texts differ between printings and a title is not a source. The Dickinson in this book is the proof: it is in the second series, not the first, and the only thing that found that out was going and looking. The Thoreau is the other proof — the version everybody quotes has somebody marching to a beat, and he wrote neither word.
  • Every line says how it was checked, and when. The public domain ones were read out of the transcription named on the card. The contemporary ones were read off our own published pages. An unchecked quotation sitting among checked ones looking identical is the thing this room is built to prevent, so there is nowhere for one to hide.
  • Nothing here is ranked, counted or voted on. No favourites, no most-copied, no top of the bank, no number on a card. A quote bank is the shape of thing that grows one, and this room will never grow a leaderboard — refusing one matters because a leaderboard would turn the one page meant to meet somebody where they are into a popularity contest between other people’s grief.
  • There are no faces in this room. A quote bank for states of mind obviously wants a row of moods you pick from, and the obvious drawing for a mood is a face. A row of happy, sad and angry faces on a Disabled people’s site is the emotion flashcard that autistic people spend their childhoods being drilled with. So each card says what it is for in a sentence about a situation you might actually be in, and you read the ones that sound like your week.
  • The quotations are set in type; only the margins are handwritten. The hand is for the keeper’s own asides. A passage set in a handwriting face is an access failure wearing atmosphere, so the tool refuses one.

The attribution slip

Got one for the book? Fill this in and it writes you a tidy block of text to copy. Send it however you already talk to us — email, Discord, a text message. Nothing on this page is submitted anywhere. There is no form action, no endpoint and no request: the button copies to your clipboard and that is the whole of it.

You will not get it all. Send what you have and say what you are unsure of — “heard it in a lecture, never found the book” is a genuinely useful thing to write in the last box, and a great deal better than a guess that reads like a fact.

The spine of this is TASL — Title, Author, Source, Licence, which is Creative Commons’ own shorthand for crediting something you have reused. It is the right skeleton for a photograph, where the thing you are pointing at is one file at one address. A quotation needs three more, marked + below: which printing, where you found it, and what it is for.

Keep it short -- this room's own limit is 30 words, and the reason is on the page.

The person, not the person who said it to you. If you do not know, say so here rather than guessing.

The book, poem, essay, talk or post. A chapter or section number if you have one.

The edition, year, publisher, issue -- whatever pins down WHICH version of the text this is. Texts differ between printings and this is the field that makes an entry checkable.

A link to the work itself where there is one. Not a quotation site.

Public domain, a Creative Commons licence, all rights reserved, or your own words -- whichever it is.

The book you were reading, the person who told you, the page you saw it on. This is the field that catches a misquotation, because most of them are copied in good faith off something that was already wrong.

A situation somebody might be in, in your own words. Not a mood to pick off a list.

Whose words these are

Not ours, mostly, and that is the point of the room. Helen Edgar’s line is hers and is credited to her by name rather than folded into our “we”. Karen Barad’s comes with its citation, as it does in the room built on it. The public domain lines were read out of the transcriptions each card names, and Meric Casaubon is credited for the Meditations because a translation is a work somebody made and is in copyright on its own clock. Two of them are ours, off our own tenets.

Our glossary quotes Seneca on learning sayings so well that words become works. It is not in here: the wording we published is a modern translation we cannot name, and the public domain one does not read that way. Better a gap somebody has checked than a line nobody can place.

The job: Which printing it was

Before the code: The Emily Dickinson line in that book is not in the series everybody assumes. Which series is it in? One word.

Hint 1

It is not on the card with the quotation on it. It is in the room's list of what the book will and will not do.

Hint 2

The rule is the one about naming the printing rather than the title, and the room calls this entry its proof.

Last hint

The room says it is in the second series, not the first.

Give me the answer

The answer is The second. It is in the Second Series of 1891, edited by Mabel Loomis Todd and T. W. Higginson, not the first. That is the whole argument of the room in one entry: a title is not a source, these texts differ between printings, and the only thing that told anybody which one it was was going and reading the title page above the poem instead of remembering. Todd and Higginson edited Dickinson's punctuation before they let her out, so the printing you name decides the words you are quoting.

Taking the answer is a real way through this, not a lesser one. Nothing is watching and nothing is written down.
