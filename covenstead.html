Covenstead

Merry meet, and merry part, and merry meet again

A covenstead is where a coven meets. Not a temple and not an institution — somebody’s kitchen, in the afternoon, with one window open and the good china out. The group is the whole of the subject. Not one wise person at the front and not a congregation: a handful of people who do not entirely agree, meeting on purpose, to check their own thinking out loud in front of each other. Not one of the cups matches. That is not untidiness, it is the subject of the room sitting on the table.

Merry meet, and merry part, and merry meet again

Read the shape of that rather than the words. It is a greeting with the goodbye already built into it, and then a third clause that undoes the goodbye. The parting is agreed at the door, before anyone has arrived, so leaving is not a rupture and nobody has to negotiate it at the time or perform a reason.

That is a better door policy than most groups manage, which is why it is the first thing on this page instead of the last. If you have ever left somewhere early and spent the entire walk home composing the apology, you already know exactly what it is worth. Here the going is assumed. So is the coming back, and the door is on the latch rather than locked.

What is on the table

Every tenet below says whose it is and when it turns up. The line at the foot of each one is ours, written for this room, and is not claimed to be part of anybody’s tradition — the same split the fairy fort up the road keeps, because a reader should not have to tell inherited from borrowed by tone alone. Where the tradition argues with itself, the room says so and does not settle it.

  • Merry meet, and merry part, and merry meet again

    A greeting that contains its own goodbye, and then undoes it.

    A traditional Wiccan greeting and farewell, in common use.

    Read the shape of it rather than the words. The parting is built into the hello, so leaving is not a rupture and nobody has to negotiate it at the time. And the third clause settles what the parting meant: going is assumed, and so is coming back. That is a better door policy than most groups manage, and it is the reason this is the first thing on the table rather than the last. If you have ever left somewhere early and spent the walk home composing the apology, you already know what it is worth.

  • An it harm none, do what ye will

    The Wiccan Rede, in its best-known couplet.

    First publicly recorded in a 1964 speech by Doreen Valiente; the wording varies between published versions. · 1964

    The word rede is Middle English for counsel. It is advice, and it has stayed advice for sixty years rather than hardening into a code somebody can be thrown out for breaking. That is rarer than it sounds and it is most of why this room exists: a great many of us have been handed rules dressed as guidance, and the difference is whether anybody is standing over you to enforce it. Note also which way round it runs. The constraint comes first and the permission is enormous.

  • The Rule of Three

    That what you send out returns to you three times over.

    Also called the Threefold Law or the Law of Return; a possible prototype appears in Gerald Gardner's 1949 novel High Magic's Aid. · 1949 at the earliest

    Contested, and the room is not going to settle it: It is not a universal article of faith. There are Wiccans who regard it as an over-elaboration on the Rede, and others who consider it a modern innovation built on Christian morality. It has also been compared to other ethics of reciprocity, including karma and the Golden Rule.

    We are not going to settle that from out here, and the reason for saying so plainly is that the flattened version is the one that travels. A tidy cosmic ledger where harm is repaid at a fixed exchange rate is a very easy thing for a website to assert in passing, for atmosphere, about somebody else's religion. What is on this table is the argument rather than the verdict.

  • First Sight

    Seeing what is actually in front of you rather than what you expected to be there.

    Terry Pratchett’s, from the Discworld books. The sentence that names it is below. · 2006

    First Sight and Second Thoughts, that’s what a witch had to rely on: First Sight to see what’s really there, and Second Thoughts to watch the First Thoughts to check that they were thinking right.

    Wintersmith, Terry Pratchett, 2006

    It is harder than it sounds, because the expectation arrives first and feels exactly like observation. This street has been caught by that repeatedly and the catches are all in the changelog: a colour that was decided and never rendered, a channel name compared against nothing, a share card built from the wrong meta tag. In every one of them the page looked right to somebody who already knew what it was supposed to look like.

  • Second Thoughts

    The thoughts that watch the first thoughts, to check that they were thinking right.

    Terry Pratchett’s, from the same sentence, and the half our sibling site built its method on. · 2006

    Second Thoughts to watch the First Thoughts to check that they were thinking right.

    Wintersmith, Terry Pratchett, 2006

    Not doubt. Supervision. And this is the half you can build machinery for, which is the only reason this site has any: the contrast checkers, the class checker, the tools that refuse rather than guess. Star Stuff makes the point that matters most, and it is worth carrying here because it is about the limit of all that plumbing: only the second half can be automated. No checker will ever tell you that a beautiful pattern is doing your looking for you.

  • If I don't help them, who will?

    That the work goes to whoever needs it, including the people who will not thank you for it.

    The ethic of the Discworld witches, Terry Pratchett’s, collected and argued at length on our own philosophy page.

    You can’t not help people just because they’re stupid or forgetful or unpleasant. Everyone’s poor around here. If I don’t help them, who will?

    A Hat Full of Sky, Terry Pratchett, 2004

    I store it in other people. It’s amazing what you can store in other people.

    A Hat Full of Sky, Terry Pratchett, 2004

    A witch never expects payment and never asks for it and just hopes she never needs to.

    A Hat Full of Sky, Terry Pratchett, 2004

    Even if it’s not your fault, it’s your responsibility. Witches deal with things.

    A Hat Full of Sky, Terry Pratchett, 2004

    This is the one that makes a coven different from a club. The witches of those books do the unglamorous half of care work, for people who are rude, forgetful or broke, and they keep a ledger that never quite balances on purpose. It is mutual aid with the accounting done out loud, and it is the reason a group that meets to check its own thinking is not merely a seminar.

Only the second half can be automated

That sentence is Star Stuff’s, and it is the most useful thing anybody we publish has said about how this site is built. Second Thoughts you can build machinery for. This street is largely made of that machinery: tools that measure contrast on the page as rendered rather than as written, a checker that refuses a class name two rooms have both claimed, generators that refuse to build rather than guess. Every one of them is a first thought being supervised.

First Sight you cannot. No checker will ever tell you that a beautiful pattern is doing your looking for you, that an argument is wrong only in this context, or that a sentence which sounds like solidarity is quietly building a category of people. Those get caught by somebody willing to be disappointed by a finding they liked — and on this site they have been, repeatedly, and the catches are in the changelog rather than tidied away.

What is not in this room

  • No degrees and no initiation. Nobody is admitted, nobody is examined at the door, and there is no inner circle with the real version in it. A room you have to qualify for is the one thing this street will not build.
  • Nothing is counted. Not visits, not how long you stayed, not how many of these you have read. A place people come back to cannot also be keeping a record of how often they needed to.
  • Nobody is converted. This is a page about what a group of practices says, by people who are not members of them, and it is not an invitation to believe anything. The Wiccan material is somebody else’s religion and is treated as such.
  • No spells, no rituals, no instructions. There is nothing here to perform. What is on the table are ideas about how to treat people and how to check your own thinking, which is the part that travels.

Whose these are

First Sight and Second Thoughts are Terry Pratchett’s, from the Discworld books, and so is the ethic of going to the people nobody else goes to. His words are on this page because they are the point of it. These sentences are the tenets themselves, the way the Rede’s couplet is — an earlier draft of this room named the concepts and sent you elsewhere for the words, which described the thing instead of showing it. Every quotation here names its author, its work and its year, and links to the edition.

What is not here is the scene. The short lines above about storing things in other people come out of a long exchange between Miss Level and Tiffany that runs to several hundred words, and that stays where it belongs: quoted properly, at length and in context, on our own philosophy page, which is the better thing to read and is linked rather than raided. tools/make-covenstead.py holds this room to its own limit — wide enough for a defining sentence, not wide enough for a scene — and refuses any quotation that arrives without an author, a work and an edition to point at.

The Rede and the Rule of Three belong to living religious traditions, and the second of them is argued about inside those traditions rather than settled. This page says which is which and declines to adjudicate, because a website assembling atmosphere out of somebody else’s faith is precisely the thing that would make this room not worth having.
