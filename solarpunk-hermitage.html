The Solarpunk Hermitage

A wood cabin on wheels at the far end of the field, bristling with panels, with sunflowers taller than the door and an aerial going up out of the quiet. This is my place. You are welcome in it, and nothing in here will ask you to say hello.

Solitude with the wire still attached

Every other room on this street is a dark ground with light put on it. This one is the morning. That is not a mood — a cabin that runs on the sun and reads as midnight would be an argument against itself, and the panels on that roof are the reason the reading lamp inside never gutters.

The pitch is the furthest one from the street, which for some people is the whole specification. Betsy Selvam calls it lone wolfing — being alone well, rather than being left out — and the distinction this place is built on is hers: the aerial is up, the laptop is on, and nobody out here is unreachable. They chose the reach.

Solarpunk, as our own glossary has it, is the future with humanity put back in: slower, more intentional, tied closely to the natural world. A future with a human face and dirt behind its ears.

  • The watering hole

    A workshop with three tables on the go — a herbarium, a solar bench, and a table of Star Stuff. Where you learn from the people next to you.

    The benches are in and nothing is laid out on them yet.

Those three are not decoration and they are not ours. They are the primordial learning spaces David Thornburg named in From the Campfire to the Holodeck — the cave where you learn from yourself, the campfire where you learn from a storyteller, the watering hole where you learn from peers — which our own glossary documents at Caves, Campfires, and Watering Holes. There is a fourth, Life, where you go and make something out of what the other three gave you. It is not a room in here. It is the door you came in by.

The cave

Heavy red curtain on every wall, no wood, no corners, and light that does not move — the panels on the roof pay for a reading lamp that is the same brightness at four in the afternoon and four in the morning. The candles are ornaments. This is the quiet half of a quiet place: a cave is where you learn from yourself.

Every book here is one our own library already argues from, and each one says which page of ours put it on the shelf. Press a spine and it opens. Every one of them can be borrowed — knowledge is infrastructure and access to it is mutual aid, so a borrow link is required here and a buy link is not.

The shelves

  • Parable of the Sower Octavia E. Butler

    A girl with an unbearable gift for other people's pain walks north out of a collapsing California and writes a religion on the way. It is on this shelf because the cabin outside is the optimistic version of the same question — what do you build when the arrangements stop working — and because Butler refuses to let the answer be one clever person.

    Borrow or read it →

    On this shelf because we argue from it on Octavia Butler on Positive Obsession.

  • The Fire Next Time James Baldwin

    A letter to a nephew and a long essay about what a country does to the people it will not look at. Baldwin is on more of our pages than almost anybody, usually in the sentence where an argument stops being comfortable, and a reading room of ours that did not have him in it would be a reading room that had been tidied.

    Borrow or read it →

    On this shelf because we argue from it on Allyship.

  • From the Campfire to the Holodeck David Thornburg

    Where the cave, the campfire and the watering hole come from — the three primordial learning spaces this cabin is laid out around, plus a fourth, Life, which is where you make something out of what the other three gave you. The room you are standing in is named out of this book and says so.

    Borrow or read it →

    On this shelf because we argue from it on Caves, Campfires, and Watering Holes.

  • Emergent Strategy adrienne maree brown

    Change as something you practise at the scale you actually have, borrowing its shapes from ferns, starlings and mushrooms rather than from strategy decks. Read it next to the sunflowers outside, which are doing the same thing without the book.

    Borrow or read it →

    On this shelf because we argue from it on Resilience.

  • Mutual Aid: A Factor of Evolution Peter Kropotkin

    An anarchist geographer goes looking for nature red in tooth and claw, finds beetles and horses and villages cooperating instead, and spends a book saying so. It is the oldest thing on this shelf and the reason a hermitage is not a bunker.

    Borrow or read it →

    On this shelf because we argue from it on Mutual Aid.

  • Care Work: Dreaming Disability Justice Leah Lakshmi Piepzna-Samarasinha

    Care as infrastructure built by sick and Disabled people for each other, on no budget, mostly without being asked. The book that makes it impossible to describe solitude as self-sufficiency, which is the mistake a cabin in a field invites.

    Borrow or read it → Buy it from a bookshop →

    On this shelf because we argue from it on Care Web.

  • Empire of Normality Robert Chapman

    A history of how the normal was invented, who it was invented for, and what it costs to be measured against it. The book behind a lot of our glossary, and the argument for why a person might want a field between themselves and a street.

    Borrow or read it →

    On this shelf because we argue from it on Empire of Normality.

  • Wild Therapy Nick Totton

    What happens to therapy if you stop treating the outdoors as scenery and the human as separate from it. The most solarpunk book on this shelf: technology and nature are not opposites here, they are the same argument with the fence taken down.

    Borrow or read it →

    On this shelf because we argue from it on Wild.

  • NeuroTribes Steve Silberman

    The long, badly-behaved history of autism and the people it was done to, told with the autistic people put back in as its subject. Where a lot of readers first hear that there was a story before the diagnosis.

    Borrow or read it →

    On this shelf because we argue from it on Cavendish Space.

  • Memory Craft Lynne Kelly

    Memory palaces, songlines and the physical memory techniques of oral cultures — methods older than writing, tried out by somebody who actually learned to use them. On the shelf because a hermitage full of books should also hold the case for not needing them.

    Borrow or read it → Buy it from a bookshop →

    On this shelf because we argue from it on Spaced Repetition.

The laptop

Open on the arm of the chair, showing the street you walked in from. It is the real street and not a picture of one — you can go down it from in here.

Same site, same server, no third party — and it still waits to be asked, because a whole copy of the street loading itself into a room nobody opened would mean a second intensity dial and a screen reader walking the site twice.

The campfire

Green shag, three bean bags, and one screen the chairs are all pointed at. A campfire is where you learn from a storyteller, and the storyteller here is whoever is narrating. Nothing plays until you press play, and every button says how long it runs before you press it, because that number is what lets you decide.
