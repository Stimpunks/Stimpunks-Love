The Solarpunk Hermitage

A wood cabin on wheels at the far end of the field, bristling with panels, with sunflowers taller than the door and an aerial going up out of the quiet. This is my place. You are welcome in it, and nothing in here will ask you to say hello.

Solitude with the wire still attached

Every other room on this street is a dark ground with light put on it. This one is the morning. That is not a mood — a cabin that runs on the sun and reads as midnight would be an argument against itself, and the panels on that roof are the reason the reading lamp inside never gutters.

The pitch is the furthest one from the street, which for some people is the whole specification. Betsy Selvam calls it lone wolfing — being alone well, rather than being left out — and the distinction this place is built on is hers: the aerial is up, the laptop is on, and nobody out here is unreachable. They chose the reach.

Solarpunk, as our own glossary has it, is the future with humanity put back in: slower, more intentional, tied closely to the natural world. A future with a human face and dirt behind its ears.

Those three are not decoration and they are not ours. They are the primordial learning spaces David Thornburg named in From the Campfire to the Holodeck — the cave where you learn from yourself, the campfire where you learn from a storyteller, the watering hole where you learn from peers — which our own glossary documents at Caves, Campfires, and Watering Holes. There is a fourth, Life, where you go and make something out of what the other three gave you. It is not a room in here. It is the door you came in by.

The cave

Heavy red curtain on every wall, no wood, no corners, and light that does not move — the panels on the roof pay for a reading lamp that is the same brightness at four in the afternoon and four in the morning. The candles are ornaments. This is the quiet half of a quiet place: a cave is where you learn from yourself.

Every book here is one our own library already argues from, and each one says which page of ours put it on the shelf. Press a spine and it opens. Every one of them can be borrowed — knowledge is infrastructure and access to it is mutual aid, so a borrow link is required here and a buy link is not.

The shelves

  • Parable of the Sower Octavia E. Butler

    A girl with an unbearable gift for other people's pain walks north out of a collapsing California and writes a religion on the way. It is on this shelf because the cabin outside is the optimistic version of the same question — what do you build when the arrangements stop working — and because Butler refuses to let the answer be one clever person.

The watering hole

Three tables pushed together under the long window, with something half-finished on each of them. A watering hole is where you learn from the people next to you — so nothing on these benches is a lesson, and everything on them is somebody else’s work left out where you can pick it up. Every item says whose it is and which page of ours put it there.

The herbarium

Pressed sheets under glass, in no order anybody can defend. Every specimen is here because one of our own pages argues from it — which is why there is no sunflower on the table, although sunflowers are the plant growing outside the door: we have no page about them, so there is nothing to press. Nothing here is named to species. A Latin binomial typed from memory is a taxonomic claim nobody checked wearing the one kind of authority a herbarium sheet has, and half of these are groups rather than species in any case. Press a sheet to lift the glass.

  • Dandelion pressed whole, root and all

    Low sensitivity. Comes up through tarmac, does not much mind where it is put, and is called a weed for exactly that. The first of the three sheets that sit together on this table.

    Pressed because we argue from it on Dandelions, Tulips, and Orchids.

  • Tulip one stem, one head

    Medium sensitivity, and where most people are. The sheet nobody remembers, which is its own finding: a metaphor with a middle in it is harder to weaponise than one with two ends.

    Pressed because we argue from it on Dandelions, Tulips, and Orchids.

  • Orchid one flower, mounted separately

    High sensitivity. Does badly in poor conditions and spectacularly in good ones — which is the whole point of the metaphor and the half that gets dropped. An orchid in a bad room is not a weaker dandelion.

    Pressed because we argue from it on Dandelions, Tulips, and Orchids.

  • Bamboo rhizome a length of running stem, laid flat

    Not a root. A stem that travels sideways underground and sends up whatever it likes wherever it likes, with no trunk and no centre to cut. Helen Edgar named the Autistic Rhizome after it — a network with no hierarchy, where each person is an integral part and nothing depends on anything else's permission to exist.

    The Autistic Rhizome is Helen Edgar's, at Autistic Realms.

    Pressed because we argue from it on Autistic Rhizome.

  • Moss a cushion, dried

    Take a tree as the definition of a plant and a moss fails six ways: no roots, no vessels, no flowers, and so on down. Our own zine went and checked and the list is backwards — holding your own water is the newer trick, and it is the tree that lost the older one. A deficit list is a list of things measured against the wrong standard.

    Developed after Helen Edgar's Mossy Minds & Monotropism.

    Pressed because we argue from it on Measured Against a Tree, Star Stuff zine No. 73.

  • Lichen on its bark, not removed from it

    Not one organism and never was — a fungus and an alga, or a fungus and a cyanobacterium, living as one thing so completely that it took botany a long argument to accept it. The sheet on this table that is really two sheets.

    Pressed because we argue from it on A Field Guide to Symbioses.

  • Fern frond one frond, whole

    The same shape repeated at every scale, from the base of the plant to the tip of the smallest segment. It is the picture our glossary reaches for to explain a fractal, and the reason small patterns are worth taking seriously: what you do at the size you have is what the big thing is made of.

    Pressed because we argue from it on Emergence.

  • Mycelium a mat, lifted on card

    NOT A PLANT, and on this table anyway — a fungus is closer to you than to the moss two sheets along. It grows the way knowledge does here: no trunk, no centre, many doors, and most of the work happening in the dark where nobody is watching.

    Pressed because we argue from it on A Mycelium and a Rhizome, Star Stuff zine No. 26.

  • A seed that did not come up loose, in a paper envelope

    Planted, watered, waited on, and nothing. Some seeds do nothing at all when you plant them and are not broken; dormancy is a plan rather than a failure, and the plant is waiting for a signal you have not given it yet. The only sheet here with nothing pressed on it.

    Pressed because we argue from it on Seeds That Wait, Star Stuff zine No. 93.

The Star Stuff table

All of this belongs to Star Stuff, the sibling site, and none of it is reproduced here — these are doors to it. Three of them are where the herbarium’s sheets came from, which is what a workshop with three benches in one room looks like.

  • A Field Guide to the Ways of Being Alone

    Twelve ways of keeping your own company among our Terran kin. A lone wolf is not an outcast, it is a disperser — young, deliberate, crossing empty ground on its way to founding a pack.

    The keystone of this whole pitch, arriving from the sibling site rather than from us. Read it next to Betsy Selvam.

  • A Field Guide to Symbioses

    Mutual aid written into biology: fungi feeding trees, bacteria making soil, a fish that cleans the teeth of a predator that could swallow it.

    Where the lichen sheet on the first table came from. Interdependence is the rule and not the exception.

  • A Mycelium and a Rhizome

    Star Stuff zine No. 26: how neurodivergent knowledge grows — no trunk, no centre, many doors.

    The mycelium sheet's source, and the best short description of why this site is a street rather than a hierarchy.

  • Measured Against a Tree

    Star Stuff zine No. 73: six ways a moss fails as a plant, and the finding that the list is backwards.

    The moss sheet's source, and the clearest thing either site has published about what a deficit list actually measures.

  • Seeds That Wait

    Star Stuff zine No. 93: some seeds do nothing at all when you plant them, and they are not broken. Read-aloud science for four to eight, with every source kept on the page.
