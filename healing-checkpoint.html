The Healing Checkpoint

The bed is not a reward.

The light in here comes up out of the floor, which is a thing no other room on this street does, and it is the only light there is. There is a bed in the alcove. Nobody is at the desk, nobody is going to ask what you are here for, and the bed is free — not free with something, and not free because of something. Just free, the way a save room in a game is free.

An appreciation of rest

Built out of one page of ours: Rest, in the Stimpunks glossary, where this room got its argument and most of its furniture.

Rest is not the gap between the useful parts of a life. On our own page it is a thing a society either lets people have or does not, which makes it a question about how the world is arranged rather than about how disciplined anybody is. That is the whole turn, and everything else in this room follows from it.

The second and third of those are The Nap Ministry’s, and Tricia Hersey’s name belongs in the room rather than in a footnote: our Rest entry is built substantially out of her work, and so is this. Her argument is that rest was taken from people rather than mislaid by them, and that getting it back is something done together instead of scheduled alone.

Which is why this is a room and not a feature. A burnout page would tell you what to watch for. A spoon theory page would give you a way to describe the limit. Crip time would tell you the clock is the thing that is wrong. This is just somewhere to be, with the light low, in the middle of a street where every other door has something behind it that wants your attention.

What a healing checkpoint is

In a game it is the room with no monsters in it. A bonfire, an inn, a save point, a bed you can use as many times as you like: your health goes back to full, it costs nothing, and the game stops trying to kill you for as long as you stand there. TV Tropes calls it the Healing Checkpoint. The trope is everybody’s and belongs to nobody; the room is ours.

A checkpoint in a game does three things, and this one does none of them.

  • It restores you to full. This one restores nothing, because you are not a health bar. There is no meter in this room, nothing fills up, and nothing is better about you when you leave than when you came in. That is not the room falling short of the trope. It is the room declining a premise the trope smuggles in — that a person is a quantity that runs down and gets topped up.
  • It saves your progress. This one saves nothing at all. No account, no cookie, no local storage, no record that you were ever here. The job board one floor down keeps a list of finished jobs in your own browser and says so; this room keeps nothing, and a checkpoint that remembered you would be keeping a record of how often you needed one.
  • It sits before the hard part. There is no hard part. Nothing is waiting on the other side of this page, there is no next room you are being prepared for, and the way out goes back to the street you came in by.

And here is the sentence the whole room is built to protect: rest is not for anything. Not so you can work better, not so you can come back stronger, not so you can be more use to anybody tomorrow. Star Stuff’s zine No. 58 spends a spread refusing exactly that framing, one of our games refuses it in its own code, and our Rest entry never once offers it. A room arguing that rest is owed cannot carry a single sentence saying what it is owed for, so the generator behind this page refuses that vocabulary outright rather than leaving it to anybody’s care.

Why the room is numbered 429

Because 429 Too Many Requests is the nicest thing a computer says to anybody.

It was added to HTTP by Mark Nottingham and Roy Fielding in RFC 6585, in April 2012, and what it means is narrow and precise: the user has sent too many requests in a given amount of time. Not that the request was wrong. Not that the sender was wrong. Not that anything is broken. The rate is wrong, and a rate is a property of a situation rather than of a person.

That is the difference between a 429 and a 400. A 400 says you made a mistake. A 403 says you are not allowed. A 500 says something inside has fallen over. A 429 says: this is more than can be done at this speed, and that is all it says. Which is a thing a great many Disabled people have spent a great deal of energy trying to explain about themselves to somebody holding a deadline.

And the RFC gives the response a way to be kind. Alongside a 429 a server may send Retry-After — not a refusal, a time. Come back then. The door is not shut; the door is on a different clock. Which is crip time with a header name.

Retry-After

The one amenity in this room, and it is the same shape as the spoon drawer in the Playhouse: it hands out the sentences and not the thing itself. Five slips, borrowing the shape of a 429 so you do not have to compose one from scratch when composing anything is the problem. Open one, read it, take it or leave it.

Nothing is sent anywhere. There is no form, no endpoint, no fetch and no storage in this room: opening a slip shows you some text, and the copy button — which only appears if you have JavaScript — puts that text on your own clipboard. Where it goes after that is entirely your business. The wording is a borrowing of HTTP’s shape and not a protocol; a real rate limiter is not allowed to send the last one.

Retry-After: an hour the smallest one, and the one the RFC’s own example uses 
429 Too Many Requests
Retry-After: an hour

Ask me again in an hour and I will have more of an answer.

Retry-After: later today for the thing that is real but is not this minute 
429 Too Many Requests
Retry-After: later today

Not now. Later today, if it keeps.

Retry-After: tomorrow the most useful one, most days 
429 Too Many Requests
Retry-After: tomorrow

Tomorrow. I am not ignoring you; I am at capacity.

Retry-After: the weekend for the week that has already spent itself 
429 Too Many Requests
Retry-After: the weekend

The weekend, or the next one. It will still be there.

Retry-After: no date the honest one, and the one a real rate limiter is not allowed to send 
429 Too Many Requests
Retry-After: no date

I do not know when. That is not a no forever, and it is not a yes with a date on it either.

House rules, room 429

  • Nothing here is counted. No total, no streak, no minutes, no visits, no record between one time and the next. A checkpoint is precisely the shape of thing somebody gives a counter to, and a number beside a bed would quietly make rest into a quantity you were supposed to be managing.
  • Nothing is stored. Not in a cookie, not in your browser’s storage, not on a server. This page writes nothing down about you at all.
  • Nothing plays and nothing is fetched. There is no video in this room and no sound in it, at any setting on the dial.
  • Nothing flashes. The steam over the water drifts at Regular and at MAX and is gone at Gentle, and that is the whole of what the dial touches in here. There is no flicker anywhere in this room at any setting — Club Chronic’s rule, and it matters most in the room whose entire subject is not being jolted.
  • Gentle takes away the wobble, never the words. Every sentence on this page is the same at all three settings.
  • You can stay. There is nothing you are supposed to do next and nobody is waiting for the bed.
