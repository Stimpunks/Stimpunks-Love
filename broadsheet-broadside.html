A broadsheet press on the street

The Broadsheet Broadside

Manifestos and mementos, served across your bow.

A broadside is two things. It is a large sheet of paper printed on one side and posted up where people will see it — proclamations, ballads, political views, advertisements — and it is the side of a warship, with every gun along it. Both definitions are Wikipedia’s, in print and at sea, read on 2026-09-25. This shopfront is the second, seen from a boat at the waterline, and what comes out of its ports is the first.

Ours are printed on both sides, which strictly makes them broadsheets, and that is where the press gets its name. The form is the Stimpunks broadsides’, which whysheet.press prints: one sheet, made to be carried into a room and handed to somebody. Side A carries one thing you can read across the room, to pin up. Side B does the work, to hand over. And theirs is Alfie Kohn’s, whose Why Sheet was a single double-sided sheet laying out a case briskly.

The ship’s articles

  • One sheet, both sides. Every broadside prints to exactly one sheet, side A then side B, on a page that fits both A4 and US Letter, so it comes out of a printer in any country uncropped. Print this page, or press the button over a sheet, and nothing else comes out with it.
  • Ink on white, on the screen as well. What you see here is a preview of the paper and not the paper, and it is drawn the way the paper will come out: white, because that is what comes out of a printer.
  • Two inks to a sheet, and no two sheets share a pair. The pair is what tells two sheets apart face down in a stack. Every ink is measured on white before it goes on the press.
  • Every address on a sheet is printed as the address. On paper a link is an underlined word that goes nowhere, so each one reads as the place it goes.
  • Every sheet carries its credits and its licence on the paper, because a printed sheet is the one place a credit cannot be a link somebody follows later. The sheets are CC0: print a stack and give them away.
  • A shot across the bow is meant to miss. A broadside here says what we hold and never what you must do. It makes you look, and that is all it is for. No gun is drawn on this ship, and nothing on it is aimed at anybody.

    How the street works

    Walk in anywhere. This is what you will find, and what we kept careful while we got loud.

    Where to go

    • The front door is stimpunks.world. The shopfronts run along the street from there, and some rooms have more rooms behind them.
    • Past the treeline at one end is a campground, for being a little apart from everybody. Past the last streetlight at the other is a road out of town, for staying out late.
    • The map is the whole street as a model in white card, at stimpunks.world/map.html. Every page has a Where am I? at its foot.
    • Your Room is an empty storefront with the keys in the door, kept for somebody from our community: stimpunks.world/your-room.html.

    What stayed careful

    • Attribution. A licence, not a house style. Every song, typeface, quotation and borrowed name is credited, loudly, at stimpunks.world/liner-notes.html.
    • Contrast. Clashing is not the same as illegible. The colour under every word is measured, and a pair that fails stops the build.
    • Access. A loud site that shut out the people it is for would be the thing we exist to name. So the loudness is something you hold, and never something done to you.

Whose this all is

The form belongs to the Stimpunks broadsides, and so does every number the press prints to: the page that fits both A4 and US Letter, ink on white in both media, borders rather than backgrounds for anything that must survive to paper, two spot inks darkened until they clear on white, and no display serif on the sheet, because a high-contrast serif loses its hairlines in the first photocopy. They are written up in the field guide’s notes on print. The paper size is whysheet.press’s, the largest page that fits both. The Why Sheet is Alfie Kohn’s idea, and the Stimpunks broadsides say so first.

The Nelson chequer was a colour scheme adopted by ships of the Royal Navy, modelled on the one Admiral Horatio Nelson used in battle: bands of black and yellow along the sides of the hull, broken up by black gunports, read off Wikipedia’s article on it on 2026-09-25. The ship in the drawing is ours and is no ship in particular, and it carries no guns.

The ship’s name is painted in Bodoni Moda, by Owen Earl, which names the ship and never the sheet; everything else is Atkinson Hyperlegible Next and its Mono, by the Braille Institute, Applied Design Works, Elliott Scott, Megan Eiswerth and Letters From Sweden. All three are under the SIL Open Font License, read off their own records, and on the Foundry’s shelves. The sheets are dedicated CC0, like the Stimpunks broadsides: print a stack and give them away, with no permission needed and no credit required. Every one says so on the paper and asks for its credits to stay on it anyway. The room around them is this street’s, under its licence, CC BY-SA 4.0. The full credits are in the liner notes.
