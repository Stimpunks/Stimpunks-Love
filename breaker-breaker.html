Breaker Breaker

CB radios, the way people talk on them, where they came from, and the long haul.

Breaker Breaker is a shopfront for the citizens band: the radios, the talk on them, where the band came from, and the truckers who kept their sets on. It is lit the way a convoy is at night. Every light in here is a marker lamp, which shows where the edge of something is and lights nothing round it, and that is what those lamps are for. In the United States and Canada a vehicle over 80 inches wide carries three amber identification lamps across the front and three red across the back, at the middle and as high as they will go, and more at its corners and along its sides, so anybody can see how wide, how tall and how long it is in the dark. Truckers who fit more than the law asks call them chicken lights. You see the shape of every rig on the road, and nothing of what it is carrying.

This street asks every button to say how long a thing runs before you press it. The road asks every rig to say how big it is before you reach it. It is the same courtesy.

Breaker, breaker

On a CB everybody in range shares one channel, and only one radio can send at a time: you press the button on the microphone to talk and let go to listen. So before you say anything you ask for the channel. Breaker is the asking, often with the channel’s number, breaker one-nine, and then you wait for somebody to come back to you before you go on.

Nineteen is the channel the interstate keeps on. Highway talk was once on 10 east of the Mississippi and on 19 west of it, and 19 won in most places: 10 sits right beside channel 9, which the FCC has kept for emergencies since 1969, and 19 does not. People called it the trucker’s channel. The radio on the dash in the picture is on it.

Citizens

The citizens band is named for who it was for. When the Federal Communications Commission began its personal radio services in 1945, it was to give citizens a band of their own to talk on, for their families and their businesses and for flying model aeroplanes, and the first handsets made for it sold mostly to farmers and the Coast Guard. The band everybody now means by CB, on 27 MHz, came in 1958 with 23 channels, and the 40 channels a set has now came in 1977.

That set it apart from amateur radio, the hams’ band, which asks you to pass an exam before you transmit; in the United States, until February 2007, the exam took in Morse code. The citizens band asked only for a licence you bought, twenty dollars in the early 1970s and four from March 1975, and a call sign to go with it. At the height of the craze a great many people ignored all that and made up a name for themselves instead, a handle. On 28 April 1983 the licence went altogether. The band belonged to whoever switched a set on.

That is the word this street took. Citizen is what everybody who lives here is: a room somebody brings to the street takes the street’s conventions as they arrive, which Your Room calls part of being a citizen here, and a convention anybody dislikes goes to the Town Hall, where conventions are talked over and can change. Our CB takes an invite to join, from stimpunks.org’s Community page, and once you are on it nobody is more of a citizen than anybody else. Everybody goes by a handle they chose, and nobody on the channel is ranked or counted.

Routing around the face to face

A radio you can talk on without being seen, a channel you can listen to for as long as you like before you say anything, and a name you chose for yourself: none of that was made for Autistic people, and a great many of us have found a home in it all the same, on the air and later in text. The slang

CB slang grew up alongside the ten-codes the emergency services used, and the truckers of the 1970s made it a language of their own. Here is some of it. Some of the list it comes from names people unkindly, women, sex workers and people speaking another language among them, and none of that is here.

On the air

Breaker
I would like the channel. Often with its number: breaker one-nine. Then you wait for somebody to answer before you go on.
Handle
The name you go by on the air, which you choose. What’s your handle? asks somebody theirs.
10-4
Message received. Also yes, I agree: that’s a big 10-4. Four, or foe, for short.
Copy, copy that
I heard you, and I understand.
Got your ears on?
Is anybody listening? Or, with a handle in front of it, are you?
10-20, your 20
Where you are. What’s your 20? My 20 is the truck stop at the next exit.
Reading the mail
Listening without saying anything, which is allowed.
10-10
Going quiet but still listening: 10-10 on the side.
Wall to wall and treetop tall
Coming in loud and clear. Five by five says it too.
Alligator station
All mouth and no ears: somebody who talks and never listens. A hot mic keeps the channel to themselves.
10-36
The right time, please.
10-100
A restroom break.
3s and 8s
Best wishes, borrowed from the 73 and 88 of the amateurs’ Morse code: best regards, and hugs and kisses.
Good buddy
Somebody on the channel you are friendly with. The word of the 1970s.
10-7
Signing off.

On the road

Front door, rocking chair, back door
A group of trucks running together: the front door leads and watches ahead, the back door brings up the rear and watches behind, and the rocking chair is everybody between, who can ride easy.
Hammer down
Foot on the gas pedal, all the way. The hammer lane is the fast lane.
Double nickels
A 55 mph speed limit.
Chicken lights
Marker lights beyond what the law asks for, on a truck dressed up with chrome to match.
Go-go juice
Fuel. Hundred-mile coffee is very strong coffee.
Watering hole
A truck stop. A nap trap is a rest area.
Gator
A long strip of tyre on the road, which from a distance looks like an alligator lying in the sun.
Comedian
The median, the strip down the middle of the highway.
Thick stuff
Bad weather: fog, heavy rain or snow.
Yardstick
A mile marker.
Bobtail
A truck running without a trailer.
Flip-flop
The trip back the other way, and going home.
Stack of bricks
Home. I’m heading back to my stack of bricks.

The bears

Smokey, a bear
A state trooper, after Smokey Bear’s hat, which looks like a highway patrol’s.
Bear in the air
Police in a plane or a helicopter.
Kojak with a Kodak
A police officer running radar.
Chicken coop
A weigh station, where the scales are.
County mountie
A county sheriff or deputy.

Places

Mile High
Denver.
Shakeytown
Los Angeles.
Flagtown
Flagstaff.
Bat City
Austin.
Guitar Town
Nashville.

The long haul

After the oil crisis of 1973 the United States brought in a 55 mph speed limit across the whole country, and fuel was short and rationed. Truckers, many of them paid by the mile, used their radios to find the stations that had fuel, to warn each other about speed traps, and to organise the blockades and convoys of a strike in 1974 against the new limit and other rules on the trade. By the middle of the decade the sets had spread far beyond the trucks. The First Lady, Betty Ford, went by First Mama, and Mel Blanc talked on the air in Los Angeles as Bugs or as Daffy, in their voices.

Truck-driving country is the country music of all that: trucks and truck stops, CB radio, roads, weather and loneliness, and Wikipedia’s article calls it, at least partly, an oral history of trucking. Terry Fell put out Truck Drivin’ Man in 1954, and Dave Dudley, Red Sovine and Jerry Reed are in it. So is C. W. McCall, a truck-driving country singer whom Bill Fries, an art director at an Omaha advertising agency, made up for a series of bread commercials and recorded with Chip Davis. Convoy went to number one in January 1976. Fries retired to Ouray, Colorado, and was its mayor from 1986 to 1992.

Dale Watson put out The Truckin’ Sessions in 1998, a whole record of truck-driving songs, and two more after it. He went to truck driving school around the time his first album came out, got his commercial driver’s licence, and often drives the band’s bus on tour.

What this cab will not do

  • Nothing blinks. Every lamp in here is steady: no hazards, no turn signals, nothing that flashes at any setting of the dial, and nothing moves at all.
  • No song’s words. Convoy is a story told in CB talk, and it is McCall’s and Davis’s to tell. The rack plays it, and the page quotes none of it.
  • No slang that names people unkindly, however much of the list it leaves off.
  • Nobody counted. Nobody on a CB ever knew how many were listening, and nobody here does either.
