Thrown, not planted

Wide-Spread Chaos Gardens

Food and flowers from seed thrown on a windy day and left to come up how it likes, with a bench in the middle of it.

It is a bright, clear day with a wind blowing across the garden from the left, and everything in it leans the same way: the stems, the flowers on top of them, the grass, and the seed still in the air. There is no cloud anywhere and nothing in a row. Nobody decided where any of it would go: a mix of seed was thrown into the wind, what came up came up where it landed, and what did best this year is what gets thrown again next year.

In the wind

In words: a clear blue sky over a green garden with no paths and no rows. It is crowded with flowers on thin stems of every height, red, orange, yellow, pink, blue, violet and white, daisies, cups, spikes and round heads, smaller towards the back and larger at the front, and every stem and every blade of grass bends to the right, the way the wind is blowing. Little tufts of seed are blowing across the sky in the same direction.

What chaos gardening is

A sustainable landscape specialist at Colorado State University Extension put it this way:

At its core, chaos gardening is the practice of mixing a wide variety of seeds, often including leftover packets, wildflower mixes, or cut flower favorites, and scattering them over a planting area with minimal planning.

Deryn Davidson, Colorado State University Extension, “Chaos gardening: Wild beauty or just a mess? A sustainable landscape specialist explains the trend”, The Conversation, 2025-08-19, CC BY-ND 4.0.

She was just as clear about what it is not, and so is this garden. It is somewhere to grow food and flowers with very little fuss, not a nature reserve:

But chaos gardening isn't the same as ecological restoration, pollinator meadow planting or native prairie establishment.

Deryn Davidson, Colorado State University Extension, “Chaos gardening: Wild beauty or just a mess? A sustainable landscape specialist explains the trend”, The Conversation, 2025-08-19, CC BY-ND 4.0.

The land next door has a wildlife sanctuary and a wood kept for what lives in it. This is a garden, and it says so.

What is thrown

The mix is half things to eat and half things to look at, and it is checked before it is thrown, because a cheap mix can carry something that will not stay in the garden:

Some wildflower mixes, especially inexpensive or mass-market ones, claim to be native but actually contain non-native species that can spread beyond your garden and become invasive.

Deryn Davidson, Colorado State University Extension, “Chaos gardening: Wild beauty or just a mess? A sustainable landscape specialist explains the trend”, The Conversation, 2025-08-19, CC BY-ND 4.0.

To eat

salad leaves, radishes, peas, nasturtiums, kale and dill.

To flower

blanket flower, black-eyed Susans, blue flax, Rocky Mountain bee plant, cosmos, zinnias, sunflowers, California poppies and bachelor's buttons.

Never in the mix

chicory, dame's rocket, oxeye daisy and yellow toadflax, the four noxious weeds J. E. Klett, R. A. Cox, I. Shonle and L. G. Vickerman, “Wildflowers in Colorado”, Colorado State University Extension fact sheet 7.233 says to keep out of a seed mix.

What it gives

Food
Salad leaves, radishes, peas, kale and nasturtiums, whatever came up, to the café in the old dairy and the honesty box on the street.
Flowers
Bunches of whatever is out, for the honesty box, and for anybody who comes to sit in the middle of it.
Seeds
Saved from whatever did best, in packets at the honesty box, and by the handful for anybody who wants to throw some.
Connection
A bench in the middle, no paths you have to keep to, and room for anybody who wants to help throw next year's.

The food goes to the café in the old dairy and to the honesty box on the street, and the manure that feeds it comes from the farm and the sanctuary. The garden’s own panel and battery run the pump on its water butt; the rest of its power comes from the solar farm.
