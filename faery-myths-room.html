Myths & Legends

Old stones, old kings, and the folk who live under the hill.

Come through the arch. The amethyst and the amber set in it light this room, and nothing in here is a flame. It is where I keep the stories this island has told itself for a very long time: the stones on Salisbury Plain, Arthur and Merlin, the boggarts and hobs and the black dog in the lane, and the old books that wrote them down. Where a story is a story, and where something is known, I try to say which.

The Stones

Stonehenge stands on Salisbury Plain in Wiltshire, and it was built to face the sun. Press a season to turn the drawing to it.

Turn to the sun

At midsummer, standing in the middle of the circle, you see the sun rise just to the left of the Heel Stone, the big stone outside the circle, along the line of the Avenue. (English Heritage, Celestial Stonehenge)

At midwinter, looking the other way, the sun would once have set between the two uprights of the tallest trilithon. Half of that trilithon has fallen, so the view is gone, but the line is still the same. (English Heritage, Celestial Stonehenge)

What is known about the stones

  • It was built in stages: first a round ditch with banks inside and out, about 3000 BC, and the great stones put up about 2500 BC. (English Heritage, Building Stonehenge)
  • Most of the big sarsen stones came from West Woods, on the Marlborough Downs, about 15 miles to the north. That was found in 2020 by matching their chemistry. (English Heritage, 2020)
  • The smaller bluestones came about 150 miles, from the Preseli Hills in south-west Wales. (English Heritage, Building Stonehenge)
  • The Altar Stone came further still: in 2024 it was traced to north-east Scotland, at least 700 km (about 435 miles) away. (Aberystwyth University, on Clarke et al. in Nature, 2024)
  • The druids did not build it. It is more than 2,000 years older than anything written about druids. John Aubrey in the 1600s and William Stukeley in the 1700s were the ones who linked it to them. (English Heritage, Archaeologists of Stonehenge)
  • On the summer solstice English Heritage lets everybody in to the stones for free, and streams the sunset and the sunrise online. (English Heritage, Summer Solstice 2026)

Close by on the chalk

  • Avebury

    Wiltshire

    The largest stone circle in Britain, with a village built partly inside it. (English Heritage)

  • Silbury Hill

    Wiltshire, near Avebury

    The largest artificial mound in Europe, probably finished around 2400 BC, and nobody is sure what it was for. (English Heritage)

  • Wayland's Smithy

    the Ridgeway, Oxfordshire

    A Neolithic long barrow, a tomb older than Stonehenge's stones. The story, written down in 1738, is that if you left your horse there with a coin, you came back to find the coin gone and the horse new shod, by Wayland the smith. (Wikipedia, citing Francis Wise)

  • The Uffington White Horse

    the Ridgeway, Oxfordshire

    A horse cut into the chalk hillside about 3,000 years ago, in the late Bronze Age or early Iron Age. (Wikipedia)

Arthur and Merlin

Most historians today do not think King Arthur was a real person. What there certainly is, is a story that grew for a thousand years, each storyteller adding to it, and places across the land that say he was here.

How the story grew

  • About 829. The Historia Brittonum, a history of the Britons traditionally said to be by Nennius, lists twelve battles fought by Arthur, the last on Mount Badon. (Wikipedia)
  • Mid-900s. The Welsh Annals give one line to Camlann, the strife “in which Arthur and Medraut fell”. (Wikipedia)
  • About 1136–38. Geoffrey of Monmouth writes the first full life of Arthur: Uther Pendragon, Arthur's birth at Tintagel, Merlin, the last battle, and Arthur carried away to the isle of Avalon. Most of it seems to be his own invention. (Wikipedia)
  • About 1170–90. In France, Chrétien de Troyes brings in Lancelot, and is the first writer to tell of the Holy Grail. (Wikipedia)
  • 1485. William Caxton prints Thomas Malory's Le Morte d'Arthur, the great English telling that most later versions grow from. (Project Gutenberg, Caxton's note)
  • Today. Most historians do not think Arthur was a real person; he is a figure of story and folklore, and a very long-lived one. (Wikipedia)

Who is in it

  • Merlin

    The enchanter, who in Geoffrey's story brings the stones of Stonehenge over from Ireland, and arranges Arthur's birth. (Geoffrey of Monmouth, in Giles's Old English Chronicles)

  • The Lady of the Lake

    In Malory an arm clothed in white samite rises from the lake holding Excalibur. Malory has two swords: the one Arthur pulls from the stone, which breaks in a fight, and Excalibur from the lake. (He calls Arthur's sword Excalibur once before the lake too.) (Malory, Le Morte d'Arthur, Book I)

  • Nimue

    A lady of the lake whom Merlin falls for, and who shuts him under a great stone from which he never comes out. Malory gave her the name Nimue; the French romances called her Viviane. (Wikipedia, and Malory, Book IV)

  • Sir Gawain and the Green Knight

    A late-1300s poem by an unknown writer: a green giant walks into Camelot at Christmas and asks for a beheading game. It survives in only one manuscript, now in the British Library. (British Library)

Where he is said to have been

  • Tintagel

    Cornwall

    Geoffrey set Arthur's birth here. In the 1230s Richard, Earl of Cornwall built a castle on the island, very likely to make Geoffrey's scene real. (English Heritage)

  • Glastonbury

    Somerset

    After a great fire at the abbey in 1184, the monks said in 1191 that they had found the grave of Arthur and Guinevere. Historians mostly see it as a way of raising money. (Wikipedia)

  • Cadbury Castle

    Somerset

    A great hill fort, lived in from the Neolithic to the Iron Age, that John Leland wrote down in 1542 as Camelot, from what local people said. (Wikipedia)

  • The Round Table

    the Great Hall, Winchester

    A real round table hangs on the wall, but tree rings date it to the late 1200s, probably made for Edward I. Henry VIII had it painted as it is now. (Wikipedia)

The Folk Under the Hill

Some of England’s fairies, goblins and stranger things, where they are said to be found, and the old book or play that tells of them.

  • Puck, or Robin Goodfellow

    all over England

    A mischief-making household spirit who leads travellers astray and cards the wool while people sleep. Shakespeare put him in A Midsummer Night's Dream. (Wikipedia)

  • Boggarts

    Lancashire, Yorkshire and the north

    Spirits that can be a helpful house spirit gone sour, or something lurking in a field, a marsh or a lane. One old Lancashire belief: never give a boggart a name, or it can no longer be reasoned with. (Wikipedia)

  • Hobs

    northern England, the Midlands and the Borders

    Small hairy helpers who do the farm's work at night. One lived in Hob Hole at Runswick Bay in North Yorkshire. (Wikipedia)

  • Brownies

    Scotland and the north of England

    Household spirits who work in the house while everybody sleeps, and vanish for good if you give them clothes. (Wikipedia)

  • Black Shuck

    Norfolk, Suffolk, Essex and the Fens

    A great black ghost dog with burning eyes. On 4 August 1577, in a storm, a black dog was said to have burst into the churches at Bungay and Blythburgh; the door at Blythburgh still has scorch marks people call the devil's fingerprints. (Wikipedia)

  • The Lambton Worm

    the River Wear, County Durham

    John Lambton, heir to the estate, fished a small worm out of the river and threw it down a well. It grew into a monster that wrapped itself round a hill, and he came home from the Crusades to kill it, and brought a curse on his family. (Wikipedia)

  • Herne the Hunter

    Windsor Great Park

    A ghostly huntsman with antlers who walks round an old oak in Windsor Forest. The first written telling is in Shakespeare's The Merry Wives of Windsor. (Wikipedia)

  • The Green Man

    carved in churches all over England

    A face made of leaves, or with leaves growing from its mouth, carved in churches mostly from the 1200s to the 1400s. The name was given by Lady Raglan in 1939; what the carvings meant is still argued about. (Wikipedia)

  • Will-o'-the-wisp

    marshes and bogs

    A strange light over the marsh at night that leads travellers off the path. It has many names: jack-o'-lantern, friar's lantern, hobby lantern and hinkypunk. (Wikipedia)

  • Pixies

    Devon and Cornwall, on the moors

    Small folk of Dartmoor and the West Country. If they lead you round in circles you are pixy-led, and the old remedy is to turn your coat inside out. (Wikipedia)

  • Knockers

    the Cornish tin mines

    Little people underground who knock on the rock, to warn of a fall or to show the miners where the tin is. (Wikipedia)

  • Jenny Greenteeth

    Lancashire, Cheshire, Shropshire and Staffordshire

    A river hag hiding under the green weed on a pond, told of to keep children back from the water. Round Liverpool her name is also a word for duckweed. (Wikipedia)

  • The Wild Hunt

    the sky on stormy nights, all over Europe

    Phantom huntsmen and hounds riding through the sky. In England they were seen in 1127, written down in the Peterborough Chronicle, and Devon has its own Wisht Hounds. (Wikipedia)

  • Tom Tit Tot

    Suffolk

    A small little black thing with a long tail who spins five skeins a day for a girl, and will have her for himself unless she can guess his name: England's own Rumpelstiltskin, first in Joseph Jacobs' English Fairy Tales. (Jacobs, English Fairy Tales)

Story or Fact?

Each card has a story on the front. Turn it over for what is actually known.

  • Merlin brought the stones of Stonehenge over from Ireland.

    What is known
    That is Geoffrey of Monmouth's story, written in the 1130s. But stones really did travel a long way: the bluestones about 150 miles from Wales, and the Altar Stone more than 400 miles from Scotland. (English Heritage)

  • The druids built Stonehenge.

    What is known
    No. It is more than 2,000 years older than any written mention of druids. The idea comes from John Aubrey and William Stukeley, in the 1600s and 1700s. (English Heritage)

  • King Arthur's Round Table hangs in Winchester.

    What is known
    A round table hangs there, but tree rings date it to the late 1200s, hundreds of years after any Arthur could have lived. (Wikipedia)

  • Arthur and Guinevere are buried at Glastonbury.

    What is known
    The monks said they found the grave in 1191, seven years after a fire had burnt their abbey and they needed money to rebuild. Most historians think that was the point. (Wikipedia)

  • The Green Man is an ancient pagan god hidden in Christian churches.

    What is known
    The carvings are real and lovely, but the name and the pagan god idea came from Lady Raglan in 1939, and the evidence for it has since been taken apart. (Wikipedia)

  • Black Shuck burst into Bungay church in 1577.

    What is known
    A pamphlet of 1577 does tell of a black dog in the storm at Bungay. The name Black Shuck is not in it; it was first printed in 1850. (Wikipedia)

  • Amethyst keeps you from getting drunk.

    What is known
    That is where its name comes from: Greek amethystos means “not drunken”, and people really did believe it. It does not work. (Wikipedia)

  • Amber is the tears of the sun's daughters.

    What is known
    In Ovid, Phaethon's sisters weep until they turn into trees, and amber drips from the branches and hardens in the sun. Amber is really fossilised tree resin, sometimes with tiny insects caught inside it, so the story was nearly right about the trees. (Ovid, Metamorphoses, Book II, in Riley's translation)

The Wheel of the Year

Eight festivals round the year: four old Gaelic ones on the quarter days between, and the two solstices and two equinoxes. They are old, but gathering all eight into one wheel is modern, from Gerald Gardner and Ross Nichols in the 1950s and 60s, and the names Litha, Ostara and Mabon were given by Aidan Kelly in the early 1970s. (Wikipedia)

  • Samhain, around 31 October. Summer's end, when the old stories say the door to the other world stands open.
  • Yule, the winter solstice. The longest night, and the sun's turn back towards spring.
  • Imbolc, around 1 February. The coming of spring, and the ewes in lamb.
  • Ostara, the spring equinox. Day and night of equal length.
  • Beltane, around 1 May. May Day and the start of summer, with bonfires.
  • Litha, or Midsummer, the summer solstice. The longest day, and the sunrise over the Heel Stone.
  • Lughnasadh, or Lammas, around 1 August. The start of the harvest. In England it was Lammas.
  • Mabon, the autumn equinox. Day and night equal again, and the harvest in.

The Old Books

Books of fairies, myth and legend that are old enough to belong to everybody now, each one free to read online.

  • The Golden Bough

    James George Frazer, 1890, cut to one volume in 1922. An enormous gathering of magic, ritual and myth from all over the world, starting from a sacred grove in Italy where a priest-king guarded a golden bough.

    A famous and fascinating book, but Frazer's big theories, of a dying and rising god behind every myth, are now largely set aside by scholars. Read it as an old book, not as fact. (Wikipedia)

    Read it free at Project Gutenberg

  • English Fairy Tales

    Joseph Jacobs, 1890. Tom Tit Tot, Jack and the Beanstalk, Mr Fox and the rest, gathered from all over England.

    Read it free at Project Gutenberg

  • More English Fairy Tales

    Joseph Jacobs, 1894. The second gathering.

    Read it free at Project Gutenberg

  • The Fairy Mythology

    Thomas Keightley, 1828, revised 1850. Fairies, elves and dwarfs across Europe and beyond, with a long chapter on England's.

    Read it free at Project Gutenberg

  • British Goblins

    Wirt Sikes, 1880. Welsh fairies, ghosts and spirits, written by an American journalist living in Cardiff.

    Read it free at Project Gutenberg

  • The Secret Commonwealth

    Robert Kirk, edited by Andrew Lang, written 1691, Lang's edition 1893. A Scottish minister's book of the fairies who live in the hills: the Rev. Robert Kirk of Aberfoyle, a seventh son.

    Read it free at Project Gutenberg

  • Fairy and Folk Tales of the Irish Peasantry

    W. B. Yeats, editor, 1888. The trooping fairies, the solitary fairies, changelings, the banshee and the pooka, gathered by Yeats.

    Read it free at Project Gutenberg

  • Le Morte d'Arthur

    Thomas Malory, printed by Caxton in 1485. The whole story of Arthur in English, from the sword in the stone to Avalon.

    Read it free at Project Gutenberg

  • Sir Gawain and the Green Knight

    an unknown poet, translated by Jessie L. Weston, late 1300s, Weston's prose 1898. The Green Knight's Christmas game, told in modern prose.

    Read it free at Project Gutenberg

  • Idylls of the King

    Alfred Tennyson, 1859–85. Arthur, Guinevere, Lancelot and the Lady of Shalott's Camelot, told by a Victorian poet.

    Read it free at Project Gutenberg

  • The History of the Kings of Britain

    Geoffrey of Monmouth, translated by Aaron Thompson, revised by J. A. Giles, about 1136–38. Where Merlin and Arthur's life story first appear, and where Merlin brings the Giants' Dance from Ireland. In Giles's Old English Chronicles.

    Read it free at Project Gutenberg

  • The Mabinogion

    Welsh tales of the Middle Ages, translated by Lady Charlotte Guest, Guest's translation 1838–45. The great Welsh stories, with some of the oldest tales of Arthur in them.

    Read it free at Project Gutenberg
