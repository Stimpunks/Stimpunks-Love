A community green space

The Green

A wood, a pond, and a boardwalk round it, just after the rain.

It has just stopped raining. There is no sun on the Green, so nothing casts a shadow, and everything that is wet holds a line of the pale sky along its top edge: every leaf, every board, every rail, the rim of every bed. A wood stands all round, there is a pond in the middle with the last of the rain still putting rings on it, and a wooden boardwalk goes round the pond in a meandering loop, built so anybody can go round it, with beds of herbs and flowers and vegetables all along it. It starts and ends at the covered porch, where you are.

From the porch

Walk the trail

The boardwalk goes from the porch, round the pond, and back, and every stop on it has a resting place beside the boards. Here is every stop, in the order you come to them. Nothing on the trail is counted: not your steps, not the laps, and not how long you take.

  1. The porch

    Where the trail starts and ends, under a roof, with the rain still running off its edge in a line of drops. The trailhead sign is on the post by the steps, and the steps have a ramp beside them as wide as they are.

    You might notice: The drip off the gutter, and the smell of the wet boards.

  2. The herb bed

    A raised bed at the first turn of the boardwalk, its rim at the height of a seat, planted with things that smell when you touch them.

    Planted: rosemary, thyme, sage, lavender, chives, mint, in a pot of its own, because mint runs.

    You might notice: Brush the rosemary and your hand smells of it for an hour. The lavender is heavy with water and bent over the rim.

  3. The vegetable beds

    Three long raised beds along the boardwalk, every one 27 inches high, the height the University of Minnesota's extension service gives as comfortable for gardening from a wheelchair. Everything in them was planted to be eaten, and anybody may pick what is ripe.

    Planted: tomatoes, runner beans up canes, courgettes, kale, chard, peppers, lettuce, strawberries along the edge.

    You might notice: Wet tomato leaves, which smell green, and beads of water standing on the kale.

  4. The pond's edge

    A wide landing over the water, with a rail along the edge at the height of somebody sitting. The last of the rain is still putting rings on the pond, and there are lily pads at this end.

    You might notice: Frogs, now that it has stopped. The rings spreading and crossing each other.

  5. The bridge

    Where the boardwalk crosses the pond's outflow, a little stream that runs into the trees. The bridge rises one inch in every twenty on each side, the steepest the trail ever gets.

    You might notice: Water going over stones, louder than usual after the rain.

  6. The rain garden

    A shallow dip below the boardwalk where the water off the boards and the porch roof runs to, planted with things that like their feet wet. It is full now, and will be dry again by tomorrow.

    Planted: rushes, sedges, irises, marsh marigolds, ferns at the shady end.

    You might notice: Standing water with the sky in it, and the rushes bent over by the rain and straightening.

  7. The pollinator bed

    A long bed on the far side of the pond, planted for bees and butterflies. Nothing is flying yet: they wait for it to dry.

    Planted: coneflowers, bee balm, black-eyed Susans, asters, goldenrod, milkweed.

    You might notice: The coneflowers' heads full of water, and the bee balm bowed over under the rain it is still holding.

  8. The fern bed

    Where the boardwalk goes back in under the trees, a bed of things that grow in shade, and the softest things on the Green to touch.

    Planted: ferns, hostas, moss, wild ginger, foamflower.

    You might notice: Every leaf over you still dripping. The moss is cold and soaked through.

  9. The bench in the wood

    A bench with a back and arms, on a resting place beside the boards, with room beside it for a wheelchair. You can see the porch from here, and the pond through the trees.

    You might notice: The quiet, which is mostly the drip.

The trailhead sign

On the post by the porch steps. The boardwalk is built to the U.S. Access Board’s standard for trails, section 1017 of the Architectural Barriers Act standards, and section 1017.10 asks a sign at the start of a trail for exactly these, so that somebody can decide before they set off. Everything on it is true of every part of the trail.

Length
1,100 feet, one loop, back to the porch
Surface
boards laid across the way you walk, with gaps no wider than half an inch, and a strip of grit on every board, because they are wet
Tread width
60 inches all the way round, and never less than 60
Running slope
1:48 usually, and 1:20 at the steepest, on the bridge
Cross slope
1:60 usually, and 1:48 at the most
Resting places
a resting place at every stop, 60 inches long, beside the boards, with a bench that has a back and arms and room for a wheelchair beside it

The beds

Every bed along the boardwalk is raised, with its rim at the height of a seat, so nobody has to kneel in the wet to reach it. The vegetable beds are 27 inches high, which the University of Minnesota’s extension service gives as a comfortable height for gardening from a wheelchair. Everything in them was planted to be eaten, and anybody may pick what is ripe; nobody minds, and nobody keeps count.

Every plant here is named by its common name only. A Latin name typed from memory reads as if somebody had looked, and nothing on this page was looked up in a key. Everything that is eaten on the Green was planted in a bed to be eaten, and that is the only thing on this page about eating anything.

Next door

The Green is the third of the lots side by side where the buildings stop. Through the gate on the left is the Truck Stop, and past it the Run; through the gate on the right, past the bench in the wood, is the Pile.

House rules

  • Pick what is ripe in the vegetable beds, and leave the rest of the Green as it is.
  • Stay on the boards, which are where the grit is.
  • Sit at any stop, for as long as you like. Every one has somewhere to.
  • Nothing is counted: not your steps, not the laps, not what you picked, and not you.

What the Green keeps

  • Nothing about you. Where you are on the trail lives in this page and nowhere else. The Green’s own script stores nothing in your browser and sends nothing anywhere. A film on the screen loads from YouTube only when you press it.
  • Every stop says what is there in words, as well as on the plan.
  • Nothing plays until you press play, and every film says how long it runs first.

Whose it is

The brief is Ryan Boren’s, of 2026-10-05, and so is the name. The trail’s figures are the U.S. Access Board’s standard for trails; the height of the vegetable beds is the University of Minnesota Extension’s; the word petrichor is Isabel Joy Bear’s and Richard Thomas’s. Both quotations were read on our own Nature page, the first from Ira David Socol, Pam Moran and Chad Ratliff’s Timeless Learning and the second from Lucy Jones’ Losing Eden, and nobody read the books for this room. The films on the rack are their channels’, and Ryan’s pick. The type is Della Respira, by Nathan Willis, and Alegreya, by Juan Pablo del Peral of HT Fonts, both under the SIL Open Font Licence.
