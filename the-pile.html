A junk playground

The Pile

Pallets, plywood, rope, an old piano, a slide off a playground that closed, and a den roofed in bubble wrap. Everything on it is whatever you say it is.

A lot of sand with a big tree at one end and a heap of everything on it: pallets, plywood, tree stumps, an old upright piano, a metal slide, buckets, shovels, ropes, old chairs, fabric, duct tape, and pots of tempera paint, which has got on most of it. In the middle is a den, pallets stood on end for walls and a roof of bubble wrap, and every bubble throws a ring of light on the sand under it. The light has no colour in it, anywhere on the Pile: all the colour is paint.

On the Pile

Make something

Take anything off the Pile and say what it is now. A pallet can be a wall or a raft or a ladder, and a bucket can be a drum or a hat, and nobody here tells you which. Paint the last thing you put down, or knock it all down and start again. Nothing you make is kept, and nothing is scored.

Where junk playgrounds come from

  • The idea is a Danish landscape architect's, Carl Theodor Sørensen's, who wrote in 1931 about a junk playground where children could make their own world out of what was lying about. (Adventure playground, Wikipedia)
  • The first was set up in Emdrup, in Denmark, during the German occupation. Wikipedia's article on adventure playgrounds says 1943, and its article on Sørensen says 1940; we have not read anything that settles it. (Adventure playground, Wikipedia)
  • Lady Allen of Hurtwood, a landscape architect, brought the idea to Britain after the war, to the bomb sites, in a book of 1946 called Why Not Use Our Bomb Sites Like This? There the name changed from junk playground to adventure playground, to make it easier for councils to say yes to. (Adventure playground, Wikipedia)
  • They are staffed by playworkers, and playwork, in Wikipedia's words, is the work of creating and maintaining spaces for children to play. (Playwork, Wikipedia)
  • The one at the Berkeley Marina opened in 1979 and is still there. (Adventure Playground, the City of Berkeley)
  • The Land, at Plas Madoc in Wales, seven miles south of Wrexham, has been open since 2011, and Erin Davis made a film about it, The Land, in 2015. (The Land (adventure playground), Wikipedia, and Erin Davis's page for her film The Land (2015))
  • The Yard, on Governors Island, is New York's only adventure playground, and grown-ups stay outside the main area so the children can play their own way. (The Yard, play:groundNYC)
  • The idea under all of them has a name, the theory of loose parts, from the artist Simon Nicholson in 1971. (Simon Nicholson, Wikipedia)

Simon Nicholson’s sentence is the one the theory is quoted by:

The Pile is for anybody, at any age. Nothing on it has been made safe, and nothing has been made dangerous on purpose: how high you climb is yours to decide.

Next door

The Pile is the lot past the Green, where the buildings stop. Through the gate on the left is the Green, and past it the Truck Stop and the Run.

House rules

  • Anything on the Pile can be anything.
  • Nobody tells anybody what to build, or how, or whether it was a good idea.
  • Take it apart when you are done, or leave it for whoever comes next.
  • Nothing is scored: not what you made, not how high you got, and not you.

What the Pile keeps

  • Nothing about you. What you made lives in this page and nowhere else, and is knocked down when you leave it. The Pile’s own script stores nothing in your browser and sends nothing anywhere. A film on the telly loads from YouTube only when you press it.
  • Every part says what it is like in words, as well as in its drawing.
  • Nothing plays until you press play, and every film says how long it runs first.
