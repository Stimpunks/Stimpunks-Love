A community kitchen

Hey, Good Cookin’

No one is turned away at our kitchen.

A hall with every pot on, on a cold day. The steam off the line has fogged the windows from one end to the other, so the daylight comes in soft along that whole wall and lands on nothing in particular. The only sharp light in here is where a drop has run down the glass and cleared a line you can see the day through. There is no lamp over the food: the pans on the line sit in hot water, which is what keeps them warm, and where all that steam comes from.

Come in

Come to the community kitchen if you’re hungry. Come if you’re curious about cooking. Whether hungry for food or hungry for knowledge, no one is turned away at our kitchen.

Here, you are fed with no strings attached. You don’t have to pay. You don’t have to volunteer. You can just come and eat and be. Cafeteria style self-service, with sit-down restaurant-style seating so you can sit alone, together with friends, or together with strangers who might become friends. Seat yourself. Just look for the communication badge placards at each table.

— Ryan Boren, for the kitchen

No strings

Nothing is asked of you here: not money, not an hour at the sink, not your name, not a reason. There is no jar by the trays and no rota on the wall, and nobody asks whether you really need it. Eat, sit as long as you like, and go when you are ready.

The other place on this street where nothing costs anything is Nothing For Sale, out on the road past the last streetlight: a free market in a lay-by, where everything on the tables is free to take.

Seat yourself

The placards are the interaction badges our community already wears at events, set on a table instead of pinned to a shirt. Each one says how open the table is to company, so nobody at it has to say so out loud, again and again, to everybody who walks up. Colour is never alone: every placard carries a shape and a word as well, so it reads in grey, in bad light, and for anybody who does not see colour the way it was printed.

  • Green · circle

    open community seating where strangers are welcome, you don’t have to ask, just sit wherever there’s a space

  • Yellow · triangle

    if you know one of the folks already seated, you can ask to join

  • Red · square

    please don’t sit here, I want to be alone

And the rules for them, in Ryan’s words too:

  1. If a table doesn’t have a placard, pick one up at the station at the front, and sit it on the table.
  2. If you’re last to leave a table, return the placard to the placard station.
  3. Please don’t red placard a large table. They are for 2-top and 4-top tables. Fill the 2-top tables first.

A table with no placard on it is a table nobody is at yet. A placard is about that table at that meal, never about the person behind it, and it can change: swap it at the station whenever you like, and nobody needs to know why.

The line

Cafeteria style: take a tray from the shelf at the start, slide it along, help yourself, and seat yourself. Every card says what is in the dish, whether it is vegan or vegetarian or which animal it came from, and which of the nine major food allergens it has in it: the nine the FDA names, which are milk, eggs, fish, crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soy and sesame. Gluten is on the card too, because barley and rye carry it as well as wheat.

Nothing here is certified halal or kosher. Every dish with meat in it says which animal, so you can choose, and pork is only ever in a pan that says pork. Everything is cooked in one kitchen, so a dish that does not list an allergen may still have been near one: if an allergy is serious, say so at the line, and somebody will tell you what has been near what. Every cheese in a vegetarian dish is made with a rennet that came from no animal.

There are no calorie counts, and nothing on a card says what a dish will do for you. Food is food. Nobody here says picky, and nobody is told to finish anything. Anybody who needs their food kept further apart than the plain things below is welcome at Samefood Cafe, a few doors along, and anybody who would rather build their own bowl out of a plant-based pantry, at Vital Plant Living.

The shelf at the start of the line

Take what you need from it. Nobody asks why.

  • Trays, plates, bowls and cutlery, at the height of a seat as well as a standing hand.
  • Cutlery with fat handles, and cutlery with weight in it.
  • Plates with a raised edge to push food up against.
  • Cups with lids, and bendy straws, paper and plastic.
  • A tray with a lip all round, to carry across your knees.

The line from a chair

The line and every table are built to work from a wheelchair. Every number here is out of the 2010 ADA Standards for Accessible Design, as the U.S. Access Board publishes them, and each one links to its section there.

  • The tray slide runs the whole length of the line, with its top between 28 and 34 inches (710 and 865 mm) off the floor, so a tray slides along at the height of a seat. ADA 904
  • Every pan, shelf and dispenser on the line is in reach from a seat: nothing higher than 48 inches (1220 mm) and nothing lower than 15 inches (380 mm). The standard asks this of at least half of each kind of self-service shelf. Here it is all of them. ADA 308, ADA 227
  • Every table top is between 28 and 34 inches (710 and 865 mm) high, with clear space under it up to 27 inches (685 mm) and at least 30 inches (760 mm) wide, for knees and a wheelchair. ADA 902, ADA 306
  • Every aisle is at least 36 inches (915 mm) wide. ADA 403
  • There is room to turn a wheelchair right round, a circle 60 inches (1525 mm) across, at both ends of the line and by the placard station. ADA 304
  • The standard asks for at least one seat in twenty at a dining surface to be built this way. Here it is every seat, so there is no wheelchair table, and nobody has to sit wherever the wheelchair seat happens to be. ADA 226

And what no standard measures:

  • Help on the line is offered, never assumed. Ask for a hand with a tray, a ladle or a lid, and somebody gives it; nobody takes your tray off you to be kind.
  • If the line is more than you have in you today — standing, waiting, carrying, choosing quickly — sit down anywhere and say so, and somebody brings the week’s board, and then the plate.
  • At every table one place has no chair in it, and every chair lifts out, so anybody can sit anywhere.
  • The whole week is on this page, so you can choose before you arrive rather than at the counter with people waiting behind you.

The teaching kitchen

Hungry for knowledge counts too. The teaching kitchen is at the back: a counter at table height with room under it for knees and a wheelchair, a mirror angled over it so everybody can see the cook’s hands, and a screen beside the mirror. There is no sign-up, and nobody takes a register. Every class is written here in full, to follow at your own pace, in here or at home. Each one says how long it takes before you start, what it sounds and smells like, and that all of it can be done sitting down. Nothing is graded, and nothing ends in a certificate.

Hold a knife, or don’t

An onion, a carrot and a pepper cut small, which is how most things start

Hands on
15 to 20 minutes
In all
15 to 20 minutes
Sitting down
All of it. The teaching counter is at table height, with room under it for knees and a wheelchair.
What it is like
The knife on the board, a steady knock. Cut onion stings the eyes and nose for a few minutes; goggles hang on a hook by the counter, and nobody says a word about them.

You need: A cutting board on a damp cloth, and a sharp knife: a blunt one needs more force, and force is what makes a knife slip. Or kitchen scissors, or a chopper you press down on.

What goes in: An onion, a carrot, a pepper.

  1. Put the board on a damp cloth so it stays where you put it.
  2. Cut the onion in half from the root to the tip and peel off the papery skin. Lay each half flat side down: anything lying on a flat side cannot roll.
  3. Curl the fingertips of the hand holding it under, knuckles forward, so the flat of the blade rests against your knuckles and your fingertips are behind them. That is the claw.
  4. Cut by pushing the knife forward and down, and move the claw back a little after every cut.
  5. For something round, like a carrot, cut a thin slice off one side first, so it lies flat. Then do the same.
  6. If a knife is not for you today, kitchen scissors cut spring onions, herbs and peppers well, and a chopper you press down on does an onion in seconds. Frozen chopped onion and bags of cut vegetables count as cooking. Nobody checks.

A pot of rice, and a pot of beans

Plain rice for four, and black beans from a tin or from dry

Hands on
10 minutes
In all
rice in 30 minutes; beans from dry take a night’s soak and 1 to 2 hours more
Sitting down
All of it. The teaching counter is at table height, with room under it for knees and a wheelchair.
What it is like
A quiet one. A lid that rattles means the heat is too high, which is the pot telling you to turn it down.

You need: A pot with a lid that fits, a sieve, and a fork.

What goes in: 1 cup of long-grain white rice, 1½ cups of water, salt. A tin of black beans, or a cup of dried ones. Oil, garlic, cumin.

  1. Rinse the rice in a sieve under the tap until the water runs nearly clear.
  2. Put it in the pot with the water and a pinch of salt, and bring it to the boil.
  3. Turn the heat to its lowest, put the lid on, and leave it for 15 minutes without lifting the lid.
  4. Take it off the heat and leave it covered for 10 minutes more, then loosen it with a fork. Brown rice wants more water and about twice as long; its bag says how much.
  5. Beans from a tin: drain and rinse them, and warm them in a pot with a splash of water, a little oil, salt, a crushed clove of garlic and a pinch of cumin.
  6. Beans from dry: soak them overnight in plenty of water, drain them, cover them with fresh water by a couple of inches, and simmer them until they are soft all the way through, which takes 1 to 2 hours depending on the bean. Salt them near the end.

Soup out of what is there

A big pot of vegetable soup, about six bowls

Hands on
20 minutes
In all
50 minutes
Sitting down
All of it. The teaching counter is at table height, with room under it for knees and a wheelchair.
What it is like
Onions in oil hiss, then go quiet and sweet-smelling. The blender at the end is loud for about a minute; skip it and the soup is a chunky one, which is also soup.

You need: A big pot, a wooden spoon, and a stick blender if you want it smooth.

What goes in: 2 tablespoons of oil, an onion, 2 carrots, 2 sticks of celery, 2 cloves of garlic, 2 potatoes, a tin of chopped tomatoes, 4 cups (1 litre) of vegetable stock, salt and pepper, and whatever else is in the drawer.

  1. Chop the onion, carrots and celery small, and cook them in the oil over a medium heat with a pinch of salt for 8 to 10 minutes, stirring now and then, until they are soft but not brown.
  2. Add the garlic, chopped, and cook it for a minute.
  3. Add the potatoes in small cubes, the tomatoes and the stock. Bring it to the boil, then turn it down and simmer it for 20 to 25 minutes, until a piece of potato is soft when you poke it.
  4. Taste it. A soup tastes flat until it has enough salt: add a little, stir, taste, and add again.
  5. Blend it, or don’t.
  6. Soup keeps in the fridge for a few days. Reheat it until it is steaming hot all the way through.

Another way: With chicken: stir in cooked chicken at the end and heat the soup until it is steaming hot all the way through, to 165°F (74°C).

Safe temperatures, from FoodSafety.gov: Leftovers, of any kind: 165°F (74°C); Chicken, turkey and other poultry, all of it: 165°F (74°C).

One tray, one oven, and a chair

Roast vegetables with chickpeas, or with chicken thighs, for two to four

Hands on
15 minutes
In all
45 minutes
Sitting down
All of it. The teaching counter is at table height, with room under it for knees and a wheelchair.
What it is like
The oven ticks as it heats, and the tray spits a little. It smells of roasting from about twenty minutes in. Oven gloves that cover the forearm hang on the hook by the oven.

You need: A big baking tray, oven gloves, and a food thermometer for the chicken.

What goes in: 4 potatoes or sweet potatoes, 3 carrots, 2 peppers, an onion, a tin of chickpeas, 3 tablespoons of oil, salt, pepper, and paprika or cumin.

  1. Heat the oven to 425°F (220°C).
  2. Cut the vegetables into pieces about the same size as each other, so they are done at the same time.
  3. Toss them on the tray with the oil, salt and spice, and spread them out in one layer. Piled up, they steam instead of browning.
  4. Roast them for 20 minutes.
  5. Drain the chickpeas, pat them dry, and scatter them over. Turn everything over with a spatula, and roast it for 15 to 20 minutes more, until the edges are brown and the potatoes are soft.
  6. Most of this is waiting, and all the waiting can be done sitting down.

Another way: With chicken: leave out the chickpeas and lay 4 chicken thighs on the vegetables from the start, skin side up. They are done when a thermometer in the thickest part says 165°F (74°C), which is FoodSafety.gov’s number for chicken.

Safe temperatures, from FoodSafety.gov: Chicken, turkey and other poultry, all of it: 165°F (74°C).

Eggs, and a scramble without them

Scrambled eggs, or a tofu scramble, for one or two

Hands on
10 minutes
In all
10 minutes
Sitting down
All of it. The teaching counter is at table height, with room under it for knees and a wheelchair.
What it is like
A quiet one. The butter or the oil sizzles softly; if it spits, the pan is too hot.

You need: A non-stick pan and a flat spatula.

What goes in: 3 eggs, a knob of butter or a spoon of oil, salt. Or a block of firm tofu, half an onion, a pinch of turmeric, salt and pepper, and black salt if you have it.

  1. Beat the eggs in a bowl with a pinch of salt.
  2. Melt the butter in the pan over a low to medium heat, and pour the eggs in.
  3. Push them slowly from the edges into the middle with the spatula, and keep going until there is no runny egg left: FoodSafety.gov says to cook eggs until the yolk and the white are firm.
  4. For the scramble without eggs: soften the chopped onion in the oil, crumble the tofu in with your hands, and add the turmeric, which is for colour, and salt and pepper.
  5. Cook it for 5 to 8 minutes, stirring, until it is hot and catching a little at the edges. A pinch of black salt at the end makes it smell of eggs, if that is what you want.

Safe temperatures, from FoodSafety.gov: Eggs: Cook until the yolk and the white are firm.

  • The only green, yellow and red in the room are the placards, so on a table a colour always means what the placard says.

Whose it is

The welcome, the placards and their rules are Ryan Boren’s, word for word from his brief for this room. The placards are interaction badges on a table: they were first developed by Autism Network International, as our Interaction Badges page says, and the circle, the triangle and the square are the shapes on that page and on Cavendish Cards’ badge maker, which Ryan’s brief links. The temperatures are FoodSafety.gov’s, the nine allergens are the FDA’s, and every measurement of the line is the 2010 ADA Standards’; each was read on its own page on 2026-10-04. The menu and the classes were written for this room. The name plays on the title of Hank Williams’s 1951 song Hey, Good Lookin’, and nothing else of the song is here. The videos belong to their channels, none of which is affiliated with Stimpunks. Set in Corben, by Vernon Adams, and Parkinsans, by Red Stone, both under the SIL Open Font License. Every source is in the liner notes.
