Laughingstock

Tonight: disabled comics, on their own terms

Comedy is a form of knowledge. When disabled comedians take the stage, they bring a perspective shaped by daily navigation of a world that was not designed for them — and they do it on their own terms, in their own voice, controlling the frame. They make audiences do their own thinking.

Disabled comedians are not inspiring for being on stage. They are comics who happen to be disabled. And these sets map the gap between how disability is publicly discussed and how disabled people actually live, laugh, and relate to their own bodies and communities.

From our Campfire Learn Together, Being Disabled Can Be Pretty Funny, where the community watched Josh Blue and Tina Friml together and talked about what their work reveals about disability, identity, and the world.

Who is on

In this room, the only thing that stands in the light is what the comics said. Everything else — our write-up, our notes, the house rules — is out here in the dark with the audience, which is where it belongs.

Josh Blue

Josh Blue has cerebral palsy and is a Paralympian in soccer. He won Last Comic Standing in 2006 and has been touring for over twenty years. His specials center disability without apologizing for it: life with a palsy arm that “has some stuff to do” after dark, a restaurant server who asks his friend “what will he have,” getting put on the disabled list after an injury at the Paralympics, and Botox, which in his telling was developed to help people with cerebral palsy before somebody noticed it would sell.

Tina Friml

Tina Friml has cerebral palsy. She frames herself as “the bisexuality of ability” — not fully committed to either side — and names three perks of being a disabled comic: she can never be a hack (“nothing about this is relatable”), CP is the most interesting thing about her (otherwise she is just “a liberal white girl from Vermont”), and nothing she does can fail, because an audience will file all of it under inspiration. That last one is not a boast. It is a critique. She calls it being on “free play” — a baby who cheated death and has been in the bonus round ever since.

What the laughs are doing

  • “I suffer from people” — not from cerebral palsy. That is the social model of disability in one sentence. Comedy delivers it where argument bounces off.
  • Even bombing is treated as a TED Talk. Inspiration exploitation means disabled people cannot lose, and that is not a compliment: it removes disabled people from the possibility of being judged on their actual work. “Free play” is the same trap from another angle. It sounds like freedom. It is also a way of being taken out of ordinary life.
  • “Nothing about this is relatable” is not a limitation. It is a position. Comedy from genuinely outside perspectives makes an audience aware of the frame it was inside without knowing it.
  • Asking somebody’s companion “what will he have?” while the disabled person is sitting right there is not a rare failure of manners. It is a routine expression of the assumption that a disabled person at the table is not the one to address.
  • The same person with the same walk gets read as a celebrity, a drunk, or a danger, depending on who is looking. Disability legibility is not neutral. It is shaped by race, gender, class and context.
  • The disability community is the only minority group everyone will eventually join. If that is true, why is it still treated as other, and why is joining it coded as loss?
  • Being around other disabled people — where your disability is the norm, not the exception — changes what you can say, how much energy you spend, and how you move through space. That kind of community is not a luxury. It is a need.

For the bar afterwards

The Campfire ended with questions rather than answers. Three of them, to take home with you:

  • What would it mean for a disabled person to just be bad at something — unremarkable, having a normal day?
  • When have you seen humor do something that more direct advocacy couldn’t? And what are the costs of always having to be funny to be heard?
  • There’s a difference between laughing with a comedian and laughing at the subject of their jokes. How do you read the difference in a room?

The rest of them are in the write-up. Campfire Learn Together is where we watch things like this together, and it is open to the whole community.

House rules

  • There is no cover and no two-drink minimum, and nothing in here is for sale. The water on the stool is free.
  • The way in is a ramp, and so is the way onto the stage. The mic comes down to whatever height the comic wants it at, and nobody has to sit on the stool.
  • Nobody in the front row is made part of the act without asking to be. Sit wherever you like.
  • Talk to the comic. Not to whoever came in with them.
  • Bombing is allowed. A set that does not land is a set that did not land, and nobody here will turn it into a TED Talk.
  • Nobody is called brave for getting up there. Tell them they were funny, or tell them they were not.
  • Nobody headlines, either. The bill runs in the order the playlist keeps, and a place on it is not a rank.
  • Laughter is loud and arrives without warning, which makes a comedy club one of the harder rooms on any street to be in. Nothing here plays until you press it, the volume is yours, and leaving in the middle of a set is not heckling.
  • Laughingstock is the name of the club. It is nobody’s job.

Give me the answer

The answer is Your manager. “I’m guessing: talk with your manager.” The server had gone round the table taking everybody’s order and, when they got to Josh Blue, asked the person next to him. It is not a rare failure of manners. It is the routine assumption that a disabled person at the table is not the one to address.
