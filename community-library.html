Community Library

Knowledge is infrastructure. Access to it is mutual aid.

This is our library, with the doors open and the sky in the windows. Books of the edges, zine walls, music, bookmarks, and things you can borrow and have, made by and for neurodivergent and disabled people. Nothing requires a card. You do not have to buy anything to be here, or explain yourself, or be social correctly. Libraries are already the most radical institution most people use without thinking about it: you borrow something, you give it back, and somebody else borrows it. No one owns it. Everyone benefits. At the library you are a patron, not a customer.

In praise of the public library

  • It asks nothing of you

    Third places are the places between home and work that belong to everyone and ask nothing of you except that you turn up. No transaction, and no role to perform. Libraries are the third place, and especially for neurodivergent people, who get pushed out of so many other rooms: quiet corners for deep focus, tables for gathering, open floors for wandering, and nobody checking whether you are doing any of it properly. You can be there without performing being there. That is third places in our glossary, and it is Cavendish Space too: caves, campfires and watering holes, under one roof. We wrote about what makes one work for us in Neurodivergent Libraries.

  • The front desk

    Librarians are doing the work the rest of our institutions walked away from. They are social workers, tech support, a safe harbour and the defenders of intellectual freedom, often in the same shift, without much money and more and more under political attack. They also, quietly, know where everything is.

  • A palace for the people

    Libraries are infrastructure, and not as a figure of speech. They hold a town together the way its roads and its water do, except that what they carry is knowledge, and safety, and the chance of walking out as somebody different from who walked in. Eric Klinenberg calls them social infrastructure in Palaces for the People, and we think that undersells them. Built with public money. Open to everyone. No dress code and no cover charge. They are worth defending.

  • Every person their book

    A librarian in Madras wrote the whole job down in five sentences in 1931, and they still read like a manifesto.

    The fourth one is the one we would carve over the door if the door were ours to carve: save the time of the reader. A library that makes you queue, fill in a form and prove who you are before you may look something up has forgotten it.

  • Library joy

    Mychal Threets is a librarian, and what he shares about the job is what we mean by all of this. His joy is ours.

  • Where writers come from

    Libraries are safe spaces that nurture possibility, and a good many of the books on anybody’s shelves were written by somebody who grew up in one.

The library economy

Borrowing is not only a way of running a building full of books. It is an ethic: a way of sharing what sustains a life that needs neither wealth, nor competition, nor owning anything for ever. Murray Bookchin named the principles under it usufruct, the freedom to use what is held in common without using it up; the irreducible minimum, the level of living nobody falls below whatever they put in; and complementarity, differences without a ladder in them, each of us a small part of something bigger than the sum. We call it the library economy, and tool libraries, clothing libraries and seed libraries are already running on it. What if we ran more of the world this way? Ours is the Things Library.
