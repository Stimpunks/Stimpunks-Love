Liner Notes

Who made this noise. This street dropped almost every careful habit our other sites have — careful palettes, careful hedging, the balanced tone. It kept exactly one, and this is the page where it lives.

Attribution is not a house style here. It is a licence, it is the cheapest thing on the page, and it is the only one of our habits whose absence is a legal problem rather than an aesthetic one. So the credits are loud instead of hidden: scrawled in the margins of the rooms, and collected in full here.

The Den

Sixteen more facades, in the room behind the Jungle Room, and a fifth data file because they came from a fifth place: a discography rather than a page of ours. In 1976 the Jungle Room at Graceland — Elvis Presley’s own den, the one with the green shag and the fieldstone waterfall — was converted into a recording studio, and the bulk of his last two albums were made in it. Presley wrote none of these songs, so the writers are named on every cut in the room as well as here. The track list, session dates and album attributions came off the two albums’ own articles; each video id was searched, filtered to his official channels, and then checked on its own watch page — channel, playability, embeddability and length — before it went in, and matched against the released runtime so that an alternate take could not get in wearing a master’s name. Nothing is hosted by us, and every button says how long it runs before you press it.

The borrowed names

The rooms named after things other people made. None of these people or estates are affiliated with Stimpunks, and none of them endorse us. The rooms are homage, not licence — we took the shape of the idea and wrote our own words in it.

Danny the Street
A character from DC Comics’ Doom Patrol, created by Grant Morrison and Richard Case. A sentient street that rearranges itself to shelter whoever needs sheltering. The whole architecture of this site is stolen from Danny, and we would like that on the record.
Pee-Wee’s Playhouse
Created by Paul Reubens. Where the idea of a room in which every object is alive and clickable comes from.
Pink Pony Club, and Chappell Roan
A song by Chappell Roan, and now also a subroom of thirteen videos from the same catalogue. Both take the shape of the work and none of the words — not because a lyric is untouchable, but because this room never needed one to make its point. Where we do quote a song we follow how we quote: a line or two, never the chorus on its own, and the songwriter named separately from the performer.
The smial, and J.R.R. Tolkien
The Latibulum is drawn as a hobbit-hole — round door, brass knob in the middle of it, thick walls, lamplight, a hill on top — and that shape is Tolkien’s. Smial is his word for it too, which is why it is in this list rather than in a comment. The Tolkien Estate is not affiliated with Stimpunks and does not endorse us. What the room takes is the silhouette and the argument that a hole in the ground is a good place to live; no name, character, place or line of his is used, and the room is named in Latin for that reason.
Latibulum — Walkabout Saga
The word came to Ryan from A New (Very Old) Word and What It Means for Today, which is where anybody who wants the essay rather than the dictionary should go. The room checks the sense against Lewis and Short rather than restating the post, so that the two can be read against each other.
Enid Sinclair
A character in Netflix’s Wednesday. Colorful goth, and the patron saint of not matching the house.
Riot grrrl
A movement rather than an author. The zine table takes its form — xerox, ransom note, staples, free — and none of its text.
Quantum queering
Barad’s argument, carried into physics education by Alexis Buzzell and Ramón Barthelemy, and read from Autistic experience by Helen Edgar.
Esmx the Porkypine — Kaya Oldaker
Not borrowed, and in this list anyway. Esmx is the Stimpunks Foundation’s mascot, commissioned from Kaya Oldaker and ours to use; the artist is credited here, in the Arcade and on the drawing itself, because holding the rights to a commission is not a reason to stop naming the hand that drew it. “Esmx” is CC BY-SA 4.0 with an invitation attached — feel free to customise! Make variants of Esmx to represent your community — and the Esmx in the cabinet is us taking our own invitation rather than pasting in the original. Kaya also asks that anybody sharing Esmx include alt text. Esmx’s own page has the originals, every official variant and the blank template.
Sonic, and Pokémon
Named on Esmx’s own page as the inspiration behind the design, due to their popularity within the Autistic community. No character, sprite, sound or mechanic of either is used here; what the Arcade takes from them is the fact that the mascot already came from that shelf, which is the reason there is an arcade at all.
Penguin pebbling — Amythest Schaber
Not borrowed and not ours either. Penguin pebbling is one of the Five Neurodivergent Love Locutions, coined by Amythest Schaber (Neurowonderful) and documented by Stimpunks in 2022. The line most people meet it through is @brainsandspoons’: “I thought about you today. I remembered this thing about you. Here’s something I want to share with you specifically.” Helen Edgar wrote it up at Autistic Realms in 2023 — a pebble, a twig, a flower, a photograph from a walk, and explicitly not spending money. The cabinet is built on the locution and on nothing else.
Penguin Pebbling, the card game — Helen Edgar & Ryan Boren
There is already a Penguin Pebbling game and the cabinet here is not it. Penguin Pebbling: A Game of Creating Belonging, Building Connection and Understanding Autistic Identity is thirty cards across all five locutions, by Helen Edgar (Autistic Realms) and Ryan Boren (Stimpunks), 2026, with Helen’s artwork. Go and play that one — it is the better thing and you can print it. The cabinet takes nothing from it: no cards, no prompts, no artwork, no five-locution structure. The reason is a licence rather than modesty. The deck is CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 and this site is CC BY-SA 4.0; an adaptation would carry the non-commercial clause onto a page that cannot hold it, and share-alike means that is not a thing we could quietly absorb. Relicensing is not ours alone to do either — half the authorship and all the artwork are Helen’s.
What the penguins are actually doing
Adélie and gentoo penguins build nests out of stones, carry them about, and steal them from each other. The tidy romance is the popular retelling — one penguin choosing the perfect pebble and presenting it to its beloved — rather than the finding, and the cabinet says so on its own face. The locution is named after the image and is not weakened by that: a metaphor does not have to be a finding.
Otterly Adorbs, and the otters — Helen Edgar
Not borrowed either, and here for the same reason. The Stimpunks Solidarity Session leaves an otter cam running every Saturday while the conversation drifts in and out, and Helen Edgar wrote down what that was doing for her in Stimpunks Solidarity and Otters (Autistic Realms, 2026) — the otters as parallel play, a cave and a watering hole in one animal. Otterly Adorbs is built on her reading, and it is hers: credited to her by name and not folded into our “we”. Helen is at Autistic Realms.
What the otters are actually doing
The mechanics come from The Stone You Keep (Star Stuff No. 58), which took Helen’s reading to the primary literature and sources every finding. The three the cabinet states on its own face: Tinker, Bentall & Estes, PNAS 105(2) 560–565 (2008), on individual dietary specialisation — there is no standard otter; Hall & Schaller, Journal of Mammalogy 45(2) 287–298 (1964), Point Lobos, thirty instances over six days, a mussel taking a mean 35.5 blows with a range of 6 to 88; and Law et al., Science (2024), 196 tagged otters, tool use varying enormously between individuals and the users showing less tooth damage. One thing the zine corrects and this cabinet follows: the lifelong favourite rock kept in a pouch under the arm is a popular retelling, not a finding. The primary supports a stone kept and reused across successive food items within a bout of feeding, and that is all the game does with it.
Quill Drift’s one rule
That a porcupine cannot shoot, throw or fire a quill is the correction on card No. 3 of the Quillery, which sources it to Uldis Roze, Porcupines: The Animal Answer Guide (Johns Hopkins University Press, 2012). It is the cabinet’s whole mechanic and not a note at the bottom of it.

The rooms somebody else designed

Your Room has always said that a storefront on this street belongs to whoever decorates it. This is where that is kept.

The Faery Yurt — Helen Edgar
Pitch 01 in the campgrounds is Helen’s, and hers is the design as well as the words: the palette, the three typefaces, the drawings, the bookshelf, the glimmer window and every line of copy came from a mockup she built and sent on 2026-09-20. What this site added was its own plumbing — the dial, self-hosted faces, the consent record behind the photograph — and one colour, because her smallest grey measured 4.30 against the tent ground and 3.43 under the ember glow, and contrast is one of the two things that stay careful here. Helen is at Autistic Realms. She is a colleague, not a corpus; the room is credited to her and is not folded into our “we”. The small drawing of her yurt on the campground’s board is ours and not hers — it is the field’s sign for the pitch, drawn in the field’s own inks from outside in the dark, and it changes nothing on her page. Noted here rather than left to be inferred, because “we drew a picture of somebody else’s room” is the kind of thing that should be visible rather than implied in a diff.
