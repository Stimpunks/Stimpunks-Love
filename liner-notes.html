Liner Notes

Who made this noise. This street dropped almost every careful habit our other sites have — careful palettes, careful hedging, the balanced tone. It kept exactly one, and this is the page where it lives.

Attribution is not a house style here. It is a licence, it is the cheapest thing on the page, and it is the only one of our habits whose absence is a legal problem rather than an aesthetic one. So the credits are loud instead of hidden: scrawled in the margins of the rooms, and collected in full here.

Credits are about the people who made this street. What it keeps about the people who visit it, which is very little, is on the privacy page.

Covenstead

A meeting place, and a shopfront on the street: a kitchen in the afternoon with one window open and the good china out, not one cup matching the next. That mismatch is the subject of the room sitting on the table, because a covenstead is where a group meets — several people who do not entirely agree, meeting on purpose anyway.

It began as turning 03 in The Outskirts and was moved. Ryan’s call, 2026-09-22, and the reasoning is about what each area is for: the campgrounds are for people who want to be somewhat apart from each other, at peace in nature, with the street still in reach; the road out of town is for night revelry and for being as far off as you can get. A covenstead is neither — it is a group that meets regularly, which wants a premises with a door on a street where people already are. Helen Edgar’s observation sharpened the split: that everything out on that road could sit on the campsite if it had planning permission, which turns out to be a useful test for anything placed in either area.

It changed worlds when it moved rather than being relocated with the same paint on it. Out there it was a dark room lit by several lamps that did not match — the only room on the street lit by more than one source. Here it is daylit, warm and domestic, and the argument moved with it: the thing carrying several people who do not match, meeting anyway is now the china. That device is load bearing, and if the crockery is ever made to match, the room has lost its subject and kept its wallpaper.

Terry Pratchett’s words are on that page, and they are the point of it. First Sight and Second Thoughts are his, named in the Discworld books, and the sentences that define them are the tenets — the way the Rede’s couplet is. An earlier draft named the concepts and linked out for the words; it described the thing instead of showing it, and it gutted the room. Ryan’s call, 2026-09-22. Every quotation names its author, its work and its year and links to the edition, which is the habit this site keeps when it drops all the others.

What is not there is the scene. The lines about storing things in other people come out of a long exchange running to several hundred words, and that stays quoted properly, at length and in context, on our own philosophy page — the better thing to read, linked rather than raided. tools/make-covenstead.py sets its own cap rather than borrowing the Zibaldone’s: thirty words is right for a quote bank, where the danger is drift, and wrong for a room quoting a handful of named passages it is actually about. Sixty fits a defining sentence and refuses a scene.

The Rule of Three is argued about inside the tradition, and the room says so instead of settling it. It is not a universal article of faith: there are Wiccans who read it as an over-elaboration on the Rede and others who consider it a modern innovation built on Christian morality, with a possible prototype in a 1949 novel of Gerald Gardner’s. A page stating it flat, as the witch law, would be deciding a live argument inside somebody else’s religion on their behalf, from the outside, in passing, for atmosphere — and the flattened version is always the one that travels. The tool refuses a tenet marked contested that does not say what is contested about it.

The name was checked rather than assumed. Scottish Gaelic sìthean, a hillock inhabited by fairies, from sìth plus the diminutive — cited to Colin Mark’s Gaelic–English Dictionary (Routledge, 2003, p. 530) by way of Wiktionary, read 2026-09-22. That mattered because one of those two series uses this exact spelling for the fae court, and a room taking the name while claiming to owe the books nothing would have been advertising a debt it does not otherwise have. The word is older than both.

The documented half is kept apart from the folklore on purpose, because they are not the same kind of claim and a reader should not have to tell them apart by tone. Ringforts, the thirty-to-forty thousand of them identifiable in 1991, the whitethorn taboo and the 1999 campaign that saved a thorn bush from the M18 at Latoon in County Clare were read off Wikipedia’s article on fairy forts and its article on the Latoon fairy bush.

The rules are traditional and the readings beside them are ours. Every taboo says where it comes from; every line under what it is like was written for this room and is not claimed to be in the folklore. Nothing on that page tells anybody how to behave, and the tool refuses the vocabulary of advice, because a page about unwritten rules that ended in tips for following them would be a social skills curriculum with thorns on it.

Nothing in it is counted and nothing is stored. No total, no streak, no visits, no local storage, no server — which is the pebbling cabinet’s refusal of a tally arriving where a counter is the most obvious improvement anybody could propose, and one floor above a job board that does keep a list in your own browser. A checkpoint that remembered you would be keeping a record of how often you needed one. The Retry-After slips send nothing anywhere: each one is a <details> that works with scripts off, and checkpoint.js only adds a copy button that ships hidden — the Zibaldone’s slip and the guild marker’s arrangement, in a room that cannot afford a dead control.

The status code is the joke and it is load-bearing. 429 Too Many Requests was added to HTTP by Mark Nottingham and Roy Fielding in RFC 6585, April 2012, and it says the rate is wrong rather than that the sender is. The room quotes it because a rate is a property of a situation and not of a person. The bed, the alcove and the light in the floor are ours; the trope is TV Tropes’ name for a thing games have always had, and belongs to nobody. The typefaces are Quicksand and Rubik, both under the SIL Open Font License, and they are the only two rounded faces in the room because it is the only room on the street with no hard edge in it.
