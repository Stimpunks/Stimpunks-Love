Club Chronic

Punk music · punk community · a vivifying overlap

Freaks to the front.

Bring your stims and glims. Bring your pain and suffering. Bring the agony and the ecstasy of existence. Bring your authentic self or selves. Shed your masks or wear them boldly.

The scene is the thing, not the sound

Punk documentaries are all secretly the same documentary, and it is not about music. Community comes together to build inclusive spaces. Community rebounds after losing spaces. Community fights off skinheads with bats and pipes to keep spaces safe. Community learns how to DIY together. Thirty years of the East Bay, and the thing everybody in it keeps saying is that they came looking for somewhere to be, and when there was nowhere they built one on Gilman Street and ran it themselves.

That is the whole method and it is why a disability organisation has a punk club in it. A scene that has to build its own door ends up thinking very clearly about who the door is for. The social model of disability says the same thing in duller words: the barrier is not in you, it is in the stairs, and the stairs were built by somebody who could have built a ramp. Punk worked that out on the ground, about venues, decades before most of us met the theory. Our own essay on this is where the playlist got its name.

Lorne, in This Is What Trans Punk Looks Like, puts it better than any of our paragraphs: “To be a queer person is inherently a very punk thing. I think to be a punk person is also inherently a queer thing. There’s a lot of really vivifying overlap there between those two things for me.” Vivifying overlap is the phrase this room is built on. It is the same shape as neuroqueer: not two identities that happen to be in the same person, but each one making the other more itself.

And it is not a fable. The Linda Lindas were eleven and thirteen in a public library when they played Racist, Sexist Boy, and the reason that song exists is that a boy said a thing to Mila and, in her words, “we were really angry, and we decided to write a song about it.” Their tagline is we rebuild what you destroy. Punk’s inclusiveness was never automatic and never finished — it has been fought for from the inside, repeatedly, by the people the scene kept leaving out. That is the part worth inheriting.

The door policy

  • You are not admitted on condition that you turn out to be fine. Nobody checks first. The weird thing about you is not something this room agrees to overlook — it is roughly what the room is for.
  • Nothing plays until you press play. Same rule as every other room here. No request reaches YouTube or Spotify before you press something, and every record in the rack says how long it runs before you do.
  • Nothing in here strobes, at any setting. A punk club has exactly one obvious ambient effect and it can cause seizures, so this room has none at all. The dial still works everywhere else; there is simply nothing in here for it to turn up. A room whose whole argument is come in as you are cannot have a light in it that puts somebody on the floor.
  • A loud room is itself an access barrier, and we would rather say it than have it discovered. For a lot of us the gig described upstairs is unsurvivable — volume, crowds, no exit, no quiet room, no seat. A scene that lets anybody in and then builds the only entrance out of volume has not finished the job. That is why this club is a page: you can turn it off, leave it mid-song, and nobody notices.
  • Not inspiration. Nobody here is anybody’s evidence that attitude conquers all.

The rack along the back wall

This selection is not ours. The six racks, their order and their themes come from It Take a Joyful Sound on Star Stuff, our sibling site, which is an essay about punky reggae, Rock Against Racism and why a pure tone has almost no identity. Every note below was written for this room rather than lifted from that page, because two of our own sites reciting the same paragraphs makes an argument you can only follow by having read the other one. Go and read it; it is the better thing and this is the jukebox version.

Rack One

The party gets its name

Two outsider musics were told they were at the same party, and then behaved as though it were true. Every accepting scene starts with somebody naming it.

  • The naming

    Punky Reggae Party

    Bob Marley & The Wailers · 1977 · 5:52

    Marley heard white kids in torn clothes covering a Jamaican record and, instead of taking offence, took the hint. The song is a guest list for a party that did not exist until it was read out.

  • The workings

    Punky Reggae Party — Perry sessions

    Bob Marley & Lee Perry · 1977 · 8:50

    The Black Ark version, with the alliance still in pieces on the desk. Worth it for hearing a thing being assembled rather than delivered.

  • Music as jurisdiction

    Reggae Music

    Jimmy Cliff · 2012 · 3:55

    Cliff treating the music itself as the ground you are allowed to stand on.

  • Rights, with conditions attached

    Know Your Rights

    The Clash · 1982 · 3:41

    You have rights. Here is the small print. Still the most useful three minutes ever recorded about a door policy.

  • The two musics, joined

    Revolution Rock

    The Clash · 1979 · 5:33

    Reggae and punk on one record, by the band who put them on one stage.

HOW LOUD DO YOU WANT IT?

MAX GLITTER buys you nothing in here, and that is on purpose rather than unfinished. See the door policy.

Job marker — a safety pin

You have found a job marker. The Adventurer’s Guild is a shopfront on the street, and it posts jobs that send you round these rooms. Take the code below to its board and that job is done. Nothing here is scored, nothing expires, and one is a perfectly good number of jobs to do.

The job: Read the door policy

Before the code: This club names one thing it will never do, because it can put somebody on the floor. One word, and it is a kind of light.

Hint 1

It is on the door policy rather than in the music.

Hint 2

It is the one obvious effect a punk club has, and the one this club refuses.

Last hint

Nothing plays until you press play, and nothing in here does the other thing.

Give me the answer

The answer is Strobe. A room whose argument is come in as you are cannot have a light in it that causes seizures. The club has no ambient layer at any dial setting, and it says so on its own door policy so that nobody finishes the room later by adding one.

Taking the answer is a real way through this, not a lesser one. Nothing is watching and nothing is written down.
