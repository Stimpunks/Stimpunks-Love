Cozy grandeur

Scalable Feast Ballroom

A big, comfortable ballroom: chandeliers, gilt and parquet under a ceiling a long way up, and at each end canopies, tents and cabanas to go under, every one a living room with a sofa, a rug and something soft.

The ballroom is lit from very high up, by chandeliers hung so far above anybody’s head that their light lands on everything evenly and leaves no patch to stand in. It sparkles in the gilt and the crystal, the mirrors give it back, and the hall is bright all the way down. The only shade in the room is the shade you go into: under every canopy the hall’s light comes through the cloth, softer and the colour of it.

How big it is

You came in at the ceiling. The floor is a long way down, and the page goes down as far as the room does: scroll and you go down the height of the ballroom, past the cornice, the mirrors and the chandeliers, to the tops of the canopies and the floor. Nothing in the height has to be read on the way.

Straight down to the floor, if you would rather not go the long way.

Why there are tents in a ballroom

A room this big is a prospect: you can see from one end of it to the other, and everybody can see you. That is what makes it grand, and it is also a lot. So at each end there is a refuge: something over your head, something at your back, a sofa and a rug, and the hall still in view through the open side. The grandeur stays; you choose how much of it to have, and you can have a corner of it.

Nobody has to stand in the middle. Go under any canopy, stay as long as you like, come out when you want to, and go back.

The west end

Three canopies at the west end, set back against the wall so nothing comes up behind you, each with its own sofa or chairs under it and a rug to put your feet on.

  • The Striped Cabana

    A square cabana of striped canvas, rose and cream, its front tied back on both sides and its roof flat and taut, with a deep valance all round.

    The hall comes in through the stripes, so the light inside is in bands, rose and cream, across the cushions.

    • To sit on: a low daybed piled with bolsters.
    • To sit on: two floor cushions as big as armchairs.
    • Underfoot: a sheepskin rug, deep enough to lose your toes in.
    • Something soft: a knitted throw over the end of the daybed.

  • Round the low table

    Out in the hall, between the Striped Cabana and the Round Pavilion:

    • a sofa long enough to lie down on
    • two armchairs pulled up to it
    • a low table between them with a lamp on it, unlit, because the hall is light enough
    • a big rug under all of it, so the floor goes quiet there

  • The Round Pavilion

    A round pavilion of teal silk on a centre pole, its roof coming to a point under a gilt finial, and a scalloped valance round the rim.

    The silk turns the hall's light the colour of shallow water, the same all the way round.

    • To sit on: a round sofa built all the way round the centre pole, so you can sit facing out in any direction.
    • Underfoot: a round rug, thick and soft, cut to the shape of the pavilion.
    • Something soft: cushions in every colour the room has, along the back of the sofa.

  • The tête-à-tête

    Out in the hall, between the Round Pavilion and the Four-Poster:

    • an S-shaped sofa where two people sit side by side facing opposite ways, so you can be together without looking at each other
    • a rug under it

  • The Four-Poster

    Four gilt posts and a canopy of plum velvet over them, with curtains on all four sides that draw shut, like a bed with nobody's bed in it.

    With the curtains open it is a canopy; with them drawn the velvet lets in only a little light, plum and very soft.

    • To sit on: one deep armchair, the kind you sit sideways in.
    • To sit on: a footstool as wide as the chair.
    • Underfoot: a thick wool rug with a fringe.
    • Something soft: a weighted blanket folded over the arm of the chair.

The floor

Between the two ends, the parquet runs the whole length of the room, laid in herringbone and polished, with nothing on it but the long table down the middle.

The scalable feast

The long table runs down the middle of the floor and is as long as it needs to be: a leaf goes in for whoever comes and comes out when they go, so it is the length of the people at it and never longer, and nobody sits at the end of an empty table. Take a plate to any canopy; nothing in the ballroom has to be eaten at the table.

The east end

Three more at the east end, as far from the first as the room goes, so there is always one at the other end of the floor too.

  • The Bell Tent

    A bell tent of saffron canvas, one pole up the middle and its walls coming all the way to the floor, with a door flap rolled up and tied.

    Inside, the canvas glows the colour of a field at harvest, and the hall's noise comes through it softer than its light.

    • To sit on: two wingback chairs facing each other.
    • To sit on: a beanbag for whoever would rather be lower down.
    • Underfoot: a braided rag rug, round, in every colour of the room's other cloths.
    • Something soft: a pile of cushions against the canvas wall.

  • The long settee

    Out in the hall, between the Bell Tent and the Sail:

    • a settee along the wall under a mirror
    • a row of footstools in front of it
    • a runner rug the length of the settee

  • The Sail

    A sail of sage linen stretched between two gilt poles and a hook in the wall, low over a corner, open on two sides.

    The linen takes the glitter out of the chandeliers and leaves the light, green and calm.

    • To sit on: a chaise longue to lie along.
    • To sit on: a velvet ottoman to put things on or sit on.
    • Underfoot: a long-pile rug with a border round it.
    • Something soft: a quilt over the back of the chaise.

  • The borne

    Out in the hall, between the Sail and the Crown:

    • a round settee with a back in the middle, the kind ballrooms have always had, so people can sit near one another facing out
    • a round rug under it

  • The Crown

    Cornflower hangings falling from a gilt crown fixed high on the wall, swagged back on both sides, the way a canopy hangs over a grand bed.

    The hangings come round you on three sides, and the light between them is blue and cool, like the room just before you came in.

    • To sit on: a loveseat with deep arms.
    • To sit on: a heap of floor cushions in front of it.
    • Underfoot: a long-pile rug, the softest one in the room.
    • Something soft: a velvet throw and a pillow you can put your face in.

The ways of the house

  • Any canopy is anybody’s. Go under one and stay as long as you like. Nobody comes to fetch you for the next thing.
  • The hall stays bright and the canopies are soft. That is the arrangement: the grandeur out there, the cozy in here, and you go between them as you like.
  • No guest list, no dress code and no ticket. Come in whatever you are wearing; our Dress Codes page says why that matters.
  • Nobody is asked to dance. The floor is there for anybody who wants it and for crossing, and sitting on a sofa is not sitting out of anything.
  • Nothing is counted: not the people, not the places at the table and not the dances.

Whose this is

The brief is Ryan Boren’s, 2026-11-06, and in his words it starts “Big Comfortable Ballroom. Both cozy and grand. Cozy Grandeur.” It asked for a grand ceiling with chandeliers, gilt and parquet; canopies, tents and cabanas at each end for refuge at human scale; living rooms amid the tents with couches, chairs and rugs; plush furnishings and soft fabrics; and, on this page, the size of the room shown by scrolling down. The name is his. The drawings and the words are ours.

Prospect and refuge are two of Terrapin Bright Green’s 14 Patterns of Biophilic Design (Browning, Ryan and Clancy, 2014), quoted as our Biophilic Design page quotes them; the Community Center leans on the same patterns.
