Nobody is drawn, because the room is set up for whoever comes in. The lamps start off; the settings under the picture are the ones the class has, and nothing in the room moves when you change them.

What this room is for

This is an example for people training to teach: a mainstream primary classroom for a class of thirty, set up so that the room does the adjusting, and every choice in it written down with the reason and the source. It is not a specification and it is not the only right answer. Take it into your placement and argue with it.

It is built on the Cavendish Space Project, a free deck of picture cards, a way of zoning a room, and the model behind both, made by Stimpunks Foundation with Helen Edgar at Autistic Realms. Everything there is free to use, print and adapt.

Cavendish Space, The space

Five places in one room

HOW LOUD DO YOU WANT IT?

Starts wherever your device says. Nothing moves, flashes, or plays until you say so.

Cavendish Space zones a room into places, so that anybody can move to the one that fits how they are right now. The cave, the campfire and the watering hole are David Thornburg’s primordial learning spaces; the library and the habitat complete them. In a classroom of thirty they share one floor, and moving between them is a skill, never a misbehaviour. A place is somewhere you go, not something you are.

  • The cave

    The sensory cave in the far corner, for being alone, quiet and low. Headphones on the rail beside it.

  • The campfire

    A small circle on the rug in the middle of the floor, a few children and the cushions, quiet enough to talk in. It is where a story is told, too.

  • The watering hole

    The tables and the tray of loose parts: lots of people showing each other what they know, and you can join in or just listen.

  • The library

    The low shelf of books, the picture timetable and the box of instructions everybody adds to: what we have worked out, kept where anybody can reach it.

  • The habitat

    The whole room and the garden through the doors, which hold all the others, and which the class can change.

“If your room has no cave yet, that is the room’s job to fix.”

Cavendish Space, Place Explorers, written for children of about seven to eleven

Light the class can turn down

  • Daylight first, through doors glazed to the floor, with blinds for glare rather than for shutting the garden out.
  • Lamps on a dimmer, LED panels that do not flicker or hum, with a plate at a child’s height so the class can see what it is set to and ask for it changed.
  • Turned down to what the most sensitive person in the room needs, with more light brought to a table that needs it rather than the whole room turned up.
  • The cave has its own small light, a string of warm, steady bulbs that never flash, switched from inside.
  • Walls left mostly bare. Three calm sheets on the pinboard, not a display on every surface.

“Dim or diffuse harsh light, and clear busy sight-lines — a plainer wall, fewer displays, a calmer view.”

Cavendish Space, Implementation

When one child needs it brighter and another needs it dimmer, that is not a problem to hide. It is the kind of thing a class can talk through together, and the manifesto below says so.

Warmth and air

  • A thermostat the class can see, on the same plate as the dimmer, and the grown-ups asking rather than deciding.
  • Windows and doors that open, and a ceiling fan that is slow and quiet.
  • Layers allowed: a jumper on, a jumper off, a blanket in the cave, coats on outside whatever the timetable says.
  • Water in reach, all day, without asking.

“A layer, a fan, an open window, a warm drink.”

Cavendish Space, Implementation

Sound

  • Soft surfaces: a cork floor, felt on the walls, a rug and cushions, so the room does not ring.
  • Headphones and ear defenders on a rail by the cave, there for anybody to take, at any time, without asking and without a reason.
  • A quieter end of the room, away from the doors and the busy tables.
  • No bells, buzzers or shouted countdowns for a change of activity: a picture on the timetable and a word, said quietly.

“Turn the world down, or fill it kindly”

Cavendish Space, Implementation

Somewhere to be, for each of thirty

There are more places to be than there are children, and none of them is a reward or a punishment.

  • Standing desks that wind up and down, with a footrest bar to push against.
  • Ordinary chairs, at more than one height, and wobble stools for moving while sitting.
  • Floor cushions, beanbags and the rug, for working lying down, kneeling or sitting on the floor.
  • Low tables and high tables, a table pushed against the wall with room underneath, and a clipboard for anywhere.
  • Space wide enough for a wheelchair between every table, to every place, and out through the doors.
  • Moving is allowed. Rocking, pacing, fiddling and stimming are how bodies keep themselves ready to learn, and nobody is told to sit still.

“Students must be free to be neurodivergent, to act neurodivergent, to look and sound neurodivergent.”

Nick Walker, Neuroqueer Heresies (2021), pp. 152–153, as our Neuroqueer Learning Spaces Manifesto quotes it

The sensory cave

The one dark place in the room: a cloth over a frame in the far corner, with cushions, a beanbag, a blanket and a string of small lights switched from inside. Anybody can go in, at any time, and come out when they are ready. Nobody is sent there and nobody is fetched out.

  • It is never a time-out, never a consequence and never a place to put a child who is upset. A child who goes in has done something sensible.
  • It is never locked and never shut, and a grown-up can always see that it is safe without looking in.
  • It is somewhere to go before things get too much, not only after. Going in early is the point.
  • Where a class is too big for a cave each, the edges become caves: a corner, a table against the wall with room under it, a screen, the far side of the shelf.

“A retreat, never a time-out.” “a sensory space is never containment or seclusion.”

Cavendish Space, Implementation

Play

  • A tray of loose parts by the doors, with blocks under the shelf: things that can be anything, not a kit with one right answer.
  • Play counts. It is learning, and it is not earned, so it is never taken away as a punishment.
  • The play carries on outside, through the doors, in any weather.

Outdoors

  • Garden doors, step-free, onto a fenced garden, in sight of the room, so going out is a choice and not an expedition.
  • A sail over part of the garden, so a lesson, a story or a quiet minute can happen outside in sun or drizzle.
  • Going out to settle is a way of learning, not a way of missing it. The garden is part of the room.

“Enable free movement between spaces both indoors and outdoors.”

Neuroqueer Learning Spaces Manifesto, Ryan Boren and Helen Edgar (2024)

Technology

  • An interactive panel on a stand that goes up and down, so it can be reached from a wheelchair or the rug, with captions on for every video.
  • Tablets with real AAC for any child who speaks with one, always in reach and never taken away. A picture card is not a substitute for somebody’s own voice.
  • Text-to-speech and speech-to-text for anybody, for reading and for writing.
  • A picture timetable on the wall and visual timers that show time passing without a beep.
  • Headphones on the rail, for the panel, a tablet, or nothing at all.
  • No behaviour points on any screen. Nothing shown to the class ranks, scores or names a child.

The cards in this classroom

The Cavendish deck lies on the library shelf. A child shows how they feel and what they need by pointing at a picture or turning a card face up: no reading, no scoring, no right answer. Place Explorers is the version written for children of about seven to eleven, with a section for the grown-ups under it, and the interaction badges say, to anybody who looks, whether somebody would like to be talked to.

“The turn is the consent.”

  • Nothing is scored, recorded, ranked or diagnosed. The cards do not screen anybody. Why

“Broken systems, not broken people.”

Cavendish Space, Why

What this classroom will not do

  • No behaviour chart, traffic light, cloud or clip chart, on the wall or on a screen.
  • No taking away play, breaks, the garden or the cave as a consequence.
  • No rewards for sitting still, looking at the speaker or hiding how you feel.
  • No asking permission to go to the toilet, to drink, to move or to go into the cave.
  • No seclusion, no restraint used as a consequence, and no child shut in anywhere.
  • No label or diagnosis needed before something is made easier.

Universal design

Universal design means building for everybody from the start rather than adding something for one child later. The Center for Universal Design at NC State put it as making things usable by all people, to the greatest extent possible, without the need for adaptation or specialized design, and set out seven principles in 1997. Each one is in this room:

  • Equitable use: the cave, the headphones and the standing desks are for anybody, not for children with a label.
  • Flexibility in use: lamps, blinds, seats and places to work all change, and the class changes them.
  • Simple and intuitive use: one plate for the dimmer and the warmth, at a child’s height, and a picture timetable.
  • Perceptible information: captions on, pictures beside words, timers that show time rather than beeping it.
  • Tolerance for error: going to the wrong place, leaving a task half done, asking again; none of it costs anything.
  • Low physical effort: a step-free garden door, a panel that winds down, water in reach.
  • Size and space for approach and use: room for a wheelchair between every table and through every door.

Universal Design for Learning carries the same idea into teaching. CAST’s guidelines ask for multiple means of engagement, multiple means of representation and multiple means of action and expression: more than one way in, more than one way to take something in, and more than one way to show what you know. The places, the seats and the technology here are the room’s half of that; the teaching is yours.

“Inclusion is architecture, not goodwill.”

Cavendish Space, An inclusive environment

Neuroqueer learning spaces

Neuroqueer learning spaces are Ryan Boren and Helen Edgar’s project, after Nick Walker’s work on neuroqueer theory: places to learn that stop asking anybody to perform being typical, and that hand the room’s choices to the people in it. Their manifesto is a living document on stimpunks.org. What this classroom takes from it:

  • Somatic and cognitive liberty. Moving, stimming, fidgeting and resting in whatever way a body needs is part of thinking, so nothing in the room asks a child to hold still to be seen as learning.
  • Free movement between the places, indoors and out, and the freedom to leave the middle of something without explaining.
  • Clashing needs talked through together. One child’s bright is another’s too much; the class works it out in the open, with no one voice deciding for everybody.
  • Play that needs no reason. Open-ended materials, mess allowed, and time that does not have to produce anything.
  • Niche construction. The room is changed to fit the child, the way Thomas Armstrong describes it, rather than the child being changed to fit the room.
  • The grown-up as a space holder: somebody a child can trust one to one, who keeps the whole room safe and co-regulates rather than controls.

“The classroom must be declared a zone of freedom from the dominant culture’s pervasive requirement that everyone strive to constantly perform neuronormativity.”

Nick Walker, Neuroqueer Heresies (2021), pp. 152–153, as our Neuroqueer Learning Spaces Manifesto quotes it

“Conflicts between the access needs of different individuals should be negotiated in class as part of the learning process.”

Nick Walker, Neuroqueer Heresies (2021), p. 152, as our manifesto quotes it

What the research says about the room

  • Classroom design changes how much children learn. Peter Barrett and colleagues studied 153 primary classrooms in 27 schools in England and found that seven key design parameters have been identified that together explain 16% of the variation in pupils’ academic progress achieved: light, temperature, air quality, ownership, flexibility, complexity and colour. They group them as naturalness, individualisation and stimulation, the last at the right level rather than the most. The light, the warmth, the air, the seats the class can change and the calm walls here follow them.
  • Busy walls take attention away. Anna Fisher, Karrie Godwin and Howard Seltman taught kindergarten children science lessons in a classroom whose walls were decorated for some lessons and bare for others; the children spent more time off task, and demonstrated smaller learning gains when the walls were highly decorated than when the decorations were removed. That is why the pinboard here has three calm sheets on it.
  • Every child needs somewhere to be alone. Prakash Nair, Randall Fielding and Jeffery Lackney’s design patterns for schools make the case for places of solitude, where learning is taken in, and Cavendish Space credits them with the case for cave spaces in schools; David Thornburg’s cave is the same place.
  • Change the setting, not the child. Thomas Armstrong calls it positive niche construction, in Neurodiversity in the Classroom: shaping the surroundings around a child’s strengths. Our glossary has an entry for niche construction.
  • A place you own helps you think. Annie Murphy Paul’s The Extended Mind gathers the research on how people think better in spaces they can make their own.

To take on placement

Questions for any classroom you walk into, including your own. They are for noticing, not for marking anybody.

  • Where would a child go here to be on their own, without asking and without it counting against them?
  • Who decides how bright, how warm and how loud it is, and can the children see and ask?
  • How many ways are there to sit, stand, lie or move while working, and is any of them a reward?
  • Is there anything on the wall or a screen that ranks children where the class can see it?
  • Can a child get outside, to water or to the toilet, without a reason?
  • When two children need opposite things, how is that talked through, and who is in the conversation?
