Glow Wild Hollow

Every light down here belongs to something alive.

Down and into the rhizome, under the glowing mushroom in Helen’s yurt, is a hollow at night that runs down to the sea. There is no moon here and no lamp, and nothing is lit for you: every light belongs to something living, making it for its own reasons, all the way down through the air, the earth and the water, until the bonfire on the shore at the end. Most of the hollow is dark, and the dark is part of it.

Cold light

Making your own light has a name, bioluminescence, which means living light.

  • A living thing that makes its own light does it with a chemical reaction: a substance called luciferin meets oxygen, helped along by an enzyme called luciferase. (Kew)
  • It is called cold light because it gives off very little heat, and it can be blue, green, yellow or red. (Kew)
  • People have written about it for a very long time: more than two thousand years ago, Aristotle noted fungi, and the scales and eyes of fish, shining in the dark. (Kew)
  • Much about it is still not known. A light may help something get away from whatever is hunting it, find food, or find a mate. (Kew)
  • Most of the living things that glow live in the sea. (Kew)

Air

Over the hollow the night is dark all the way up, and the only lights in the air are alive: fireflies rising out of the grass, and moths, which make no light of their own, coming to their glow. Run a finger or a pointer through the air and the fireflies near it wake and rise; Wake the fireflies wakes them all for twenty seconds, and Keep them flying keeps them going until you switch it off. Each firefly’s light comes up slowly and goes down slowly, and none lights again for a few seconds, so nothing flashes. At Gentle they glow and stay still in the grass.

Fireflies

Fireflies over a wood, a field and a garden, in Greece, America and Austria, and one close up on a leaf in Japan.

  • Fireflies are beetles, not flies. There are more than two thousand kinds, and they are also called lightning bugs. (Wikipedia)
  • Most of them glow at dusk, to find a mate: some flash, and some glow steadily. (Wikipedia)
  • Every firefly glows when it is young, as a larva, which tells anything hunting it that it tastes bad. (Wikipedia)
  • Their light is cold light, yellow, green or pale red. (Wikipedia)
  • The glow-worm down in the earth of the hollow is a firefly too. (Wikipedia)
  • In Japan, parks are set aside for watching fireflies. (Wikipedia)

Moths, drawn to the glow

Moths make no light of their own. In the air over the hollow they come to the fireflies’ glow, and are lit only by it.

  • Moths make no light of their own, and most of them fly at night. (Wikipedia)
  • Moths often circle a light, and nobody is sure why. One idea is that they steer by the moon, and a light close by turns them round it in a spiral. (Wikipedia)
  • At night, moths carry pollen from flower to flower, including flowers that bees leave alone. (Wikipedia)

Down the whirlpool into the earth ↓

Earth

Down in the earth of the hollow the light is on the ground and in it: fungi glowing through an old log, glow-worms at the tops of grass stems, and things that glow back under ultraviolet light.

Foxfire, or fairy fire

The old log on the slope glows green where fungi are growing through it. Brew and Stew, out on the campgrounds, is lit the same way, by a glow growing in its own logs.

  • More than a hundred kinds of fungi are known to glow. (Kew)
  • The glow of fungi in rotting wood is called foxfire, and also fairy fire. (Wikipedia)
  • Some glow mostly underground, in the fine threads they grow through soil and wood. (Kew)
  • Nobody knows for certain why fungi glow. The light may draw in insects that carry their spores away, or warn off animals that would eat them. (Wikipedia)
  • Honey fungus is one of the glowing fungi that grow in the UK, though glowing fungi are rarely seen here. (Kew)
  • Foxfire lit the compass and the barometer in the Turtle, an early submarine built in the American Revolutionary War, until cold weather stopped it glowing. (Wikipedia)

Glow-worms in the grass

On the other slope, a light sits at the top of a grass stem and does not move. It is waiting.

  • Glow-worms are not worms. They are beetles. (Kew)
  • It is the females that glow, to bring the males to them. (Kew)
  • They live in meadows and grassland across Britain, and shine on summer nights. (Kew)
  • In the First World War, soldiers read maps and messages by the light of glow-worms, without giving away where they were. (Kew)
  • There are fewer glow-worms where the night is lit up by streetlights and buildings. (Kew)

Glowing back: fluorescence

Not everything that glows makes its own light. The lichen at Looming Rocks, out on the Outskirts, glows back this way under the lamps.

  • Fluorescence is a different thing. It is not a chemical reaction: a living thing takes in light and gives it back in a different colour. (Kew)
  • Human eyes cannot see most natural fluorescence without ultraviolet light, though many animals can. (Kew)
  • Mushrooms, mosses, lichens and some small animals fluoresce. (Kew)

Down and down through the rhizome to the water ↓

Water

Under the rhizome the hollow opens on the sea, where most of the living things that glow live.

Sea sparkle

At the foot of the hollow the sea is dark until something moves it, and it moves like water: touch it and rings spread slowly out from your finger, cross each other and run back off the edges; draw a finger or a pointer through it and it leaves a wake; hold still and the rings keep coming. Wherever the water moves, a blue glow builds up in it and fades slowly behind. Stir the water stirs it slowly round for twenty seconds, and Keep it stirring keeps it going until you switch it off. Make a wave rolls slow waves in along the shore, glowing where they break. Sound and Buzz start off; switched on, the water and the jellyfish make soft sounds, made here and never a recording, and a phone that can buzz gives a small one. Nothing here flashes: a glow comes up a little at a time and always goes down slowly. At Gentle the waves light all at once and stay lit, still, until you stir them again.

  • Sea sparkle is a tiny living thing, a single cell, that lights up when the water around it moves. (Kew)
  • It can be found round the coast of the UK, and has been photographed lighting up the waves at Dunraven Bay, Southerndown, in South Wales. (Kew)
  • Fish, jellyfish and plankton in the sea make their own light too. (Kew)

A jellyfish that lights up

Out past the waves drifts a crystal jelly, almost clear, so you only see its edges. Touch it, or press Touch the jellyfish, and it swims for twenty seconds: its light comes up slowly, it pulses every few seconds and rises a little each time, drifting from side to side and leaning the way it goes, and on every pulse a ring of light runs slowly round the rim of its bell, blue first and then green, the way a crystal jelly’s light starts blue and turns green, and flows down its tentacles. At the end its light fades slowly and it rests. Keep it glowing keeps it swimming and glowing until you switch it off. Brush through its tentacles and they sway. At Gentle it lights and stays lit, still, until you touch it again.

  • The crystal jelly lives in the Pacific Ocean off the west coast of North America, from the Bering Sea to southern California. (Wikipedia)
  • Its light starts out blue, made by a protein called aequorin, and a second protein in the jelly, green fluorescent protein, turns that blue into green. (Wikipedia)
  • Osamu Shimomura, Martin Chalfie and Roger Tsien shared the 2008 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for their work on that green protein. (Wikipedia)
  • The rainbow that runs along a comb jelly is not its own light at all. It is light scattered by the rows of tiny combs it swims with. (Wikipedia)

Fire

At the very end of the hollow, on the shore, there is a bonfire laid in a ring of stones: the one light down here that is not alive. Light the bonfire and it catches and burns for twenty seconds, then burns down to embers; Keep it burning keeps it going until you switch it off. Its flames sway and the sparks rise slowly; it never flashes, and at Gentle it burns still.
