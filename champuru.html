A table in the shade of a lane

Champuru

Mediterranean and Okinawan cooking, and what comes of mixing the two on one plate.

HOW LOUD DO YOU WANT IT?

Starts wherever your device says. Nothing moves, flashes, or plays until you say so — and in the lane, nothing moves even then.

Both shores have this lane. A whitewashed wall stands in full sun on one side, and on the other a wall of coral stone under a red-tiled eave stands in shade, with bougainvillea over the top of both and a strip of sea at the end. The table is set in the shade, and no sun lands on it at all: everything at the table is lit by the sunlit wall across the lane, so every lit face looks towards that wall, every shadow is soft and falls away from it, and the shade itself is the colour of the sky.

The lane

In words: a narrow lane seen from one end, running away to a strip of blue sea under a strip of blue sky. On the left, a white wall in bright sun, with a green arched door, a green shutter, and magenta bougainvillea spilling over the top; a thin strip of sunlight lies along the paving at its foot. On the right, a grey wall of coral stone in shade under a red-tiled eave, warmed a little by the light from the white wall. In front, in the shade, a round table with a white cloth and a chair beside it, both lit on the side facing the white wall, with a soft shadow falling away to the right. On the table, a plate of purple sweet potato, a plate of greens with a tomato, chopsticks on a rest, a fork, and a glass of amber tea.

The name

The name is the word for the dish everybody on Okinawa cooks. Chanpurū is Okinawan for “something mixed”, and the word is sometimes used for the culture of Okinawa itself, a mixture of traditional Okinawan, Chinese, mainland Japanese, Southeast Asian and North American culture, by Wikipedia’s article on chanpurū, read 3 November 2026. The sign spells it the way it is most often written in English. A table where two shores’ cooking is mixed on one plate is what the word is for.

Whose table this is

This is Ryan’s restaurant, and he set it up to eat in. In his words:

I want to create a restaurant on Stimpunks.World to hang out in when I eat. I use Vital Plant Living often, but I want another spot with a broader palette of dishes that reflects the rest of my diet.

This restaurant is first and foremost for me and my personal tastes and not a prescription for anyone else.

Ryan Boren, whose table this is, in his brief for the room.

So the menu is what he likes, from the kitchen next door’s plants out to fish and the odd bit of chicken. Anybody may sit down; nobody is being told what to order.

The menu

Every dish says what is in it before you order it, then its allergens, worked out from those words by the same engine as Hey, Good Cookin’, and every drink says whether it has caffeine, by the same engine as Pekoe and Purrs. Under each, one line on where it comes from.

The screen in the doorway

Across the lane, in the doorway of the stone house, there is a screen with nothing of its own on it. Under it is the last month from every channel Ryan took to try out, refilled every morning: cooks from round the Mediterranean, Okinawan cooking and Okinawa itself, Japanese home cooking and sushi. He winnows the list as he goes. Nobody here watched them first, and the titles are the channels’ own: what a title says a dish will do for you is the channel’s claim, and never this table’s. Any video can go up on the screen with the button under it, and nothing loads until you press one.

How this kitchen cooks

Okinawan home cooking uses a good deal of pork, and SPAM, which came with the American military, and a champuru there is as likely to have either in it as tofu. This kitchen cooks its Okinawan dishes with tofu, egg and fish, and its Greek and Moroccan ones without the lamb, because it is Ryan’s kitchen and that is how he eats. Nothing on this menu says how anybody else eats, in Okinawa or round the Mediterranean or anywhere.

At the table

  • Every dish says what is in it before you order it, and its allergens are worked out from those words.
  • Nothing on the menu says what a dish does for a body. It is food, and it is what Ryan likes.
  • Nobody counts anything here: not what is on your plate, not how often you come.
  • The menu changes when Ryan asks, and only then.
  • You are welcome at this table whatever you eat. The table is Ryan’s and the menu is his, and neither is advice for you.
