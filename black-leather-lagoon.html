An appreciation of The Cramps

Two people met in Sacramento in 1972 over a shared devotion to collecting records, decided that the obvious next step was to form a band, and then spent thirty-three years refusing every single opportunity to become a different one. That is the whole story, and it is a better model for a life than most of the ones we were handed.

They moved to Akron, then to New York in 1975, and turned up in the early scene at CBGB alongside the Ramones, Blondie, Television, Patti Smith and Suicide. Lux Interior sang. Poison Ivy played guitar, and wrote, arranged and produced a great deal of what the band actually sounds like — which is worth saying at the top rather than in a footnote, because the singer is the one who gets remembered and the guitarist is the one who built it. The lineup around the two of them changed constantly. The two of them never did. The band was formed in 1976 and was active until 2009, when Lux died and Ivy did not carry on without him.

There was no bass player. Not for years, and not by accident: two guitars, a drum kit, and a deliberate hole where the bottom end goes, which is why an early Cramps record sounds like something coming through a wall rather than out of a stage. It is one of the great examples of a limitation chosen on purpose and then defended for a decade, and if you have ever been told that the thing you do without is the thing you need, it is a useful noise to have in your ears.

What they made out of that is usually filed as psychobilly — they are credited as the progenitors of it — and the recipe is simpler than the label: 1950s rockabilly, played at punk volume, about monster movies, with no wink anywhere. That last part is the part everybody gets wrong. The Cramps are funny, but they are never ironic. Nobody in these records is standing outside the material being clever about it. They loved the drive-in, they loved the cheap single nobody bought, they loved the rubber suit, and they played all of it absolutely straight, for decades, at cost. Sincerity about disreputable things is a much harder position to hold than a joke, and it is the reason this band has outlasted most of their contemporaries.

And they were archaeologists. Half of Psychedelic Jungle is other people’s forgotten singles, dug out of a collection and handed back to an audience that had no idea they existed — a garage record from Texas, a novelty side from 1962, a rockabilly cut nobody had reissued. Their real argument, underneath the rubber masks, was that the good stuff had already been made and then dropped on the floor, and that somebody ought to go and pick it up. This street is largely built out of that same conviction, so we owe them the acknowledgement.

The Napa show, and how it usually gets told

In June 1978 the band played a free concert for patients at the state mental hospital in Napa, California. It was filmed by the San Francisco video collective Target Video on a portable camera and later released as a live tape, and it is one of the most-circulated pieces of footage they ever appeared in. There is no stage to speak of. The audience is right up against the band, people take the microphone, and nobody is managing the room.

It is almost always told as a joke, and the joke is at the patients’ expense. The standard line — you will find some version of it wherever that tape is written about — is that you cannot tell the lunatics from the band. That framing is very old, it is extremely easy to repeat, and it is the exact thing this organisation exists to refuse: psychiatric patients used as the punchline that certifies how wild somebody else is. We are a Disabled people’s organisation. Some of us have been the people in that room.

So here is the version we will stand behind, which requires no joke at all: a band went to a locked institution and played for people who do not get played for, for free, with the barrier between performer and audience removed rather than reinforced, and let those people do whatever they wanted for the duration. That is a booking decision, and it is a good one. Whether anybody in that room had a good evening is not ours to narrate — we were not there, they were not asked on camera, and every account in circulation is somebody else’s. We will not speak for them, and we would rather leave the gap visible than fill it with a nice sentence.

The door policy at the water’s edge

  • Nothing plays until you press play. Same rule as every other room on this street. No request reaches YouTube before you press something, and every record on a post says how long it runs before you do. The screen is the one control here that cannot say, because it is a playlist we keep adding to — so it says that instead.
  • Nothing here flickers, at any setting. A drive-in screen is a flickering object; that is practically its defining feature, and building one without the flicker took more work than building one with it. It has none, at Gentle, Regular or MAX GLITTER, for the reason the punk club three doors up has no strobe: a light that can put somebody on the floor has no business in a room whose whole argument is come as you are. The dial governs everything else out here. There is simply nothing in this room for it to flash.
  • None of these words are theirs. Every note on every post is ours, about what the record does. Not one line of any song is on this page, here or anywhere else on the street, and the tool that builds this room refuses a note shaped like verse. A room built on one band, whose words are half of why anybody loves them, is exactly where that rule would have been broken affectionately.
  • Nothing is ranked. There is no best, no greatest, no countdown, no essential nine. They are in the order they were made, 1978 at the top and 2003 at the bottom, and that is the only ordering principle in the room.
  • Not a costume you have to keep on. It is always Halloween here, which means you may arrive as anything and nobody asks what you came as or when you are taking it off.

The rack, on posts in the water

Nine records, oldest first. Each one is on the band’s own artist channel on YouTube unless the post says otherwise, and each was opened on its own watch page and checked before it went up here — that it plays, that it is allowed to be embedded, and how long it runs. The year on a post is the record’s year and not the upload’s, which turns out to matter: these uploads are auto-generated from a licensor’s catalogue and date the reissue that was licensed, so most of them announce 1984 and three announce 2014. Taking that at face value would have printed 1984 beside a 1978 single. The years were resolved separately, against MusicBrainz, on 2026-09-22.

  • THE FIRST ONE

    Human Fly

    Gravest Hits · 1978 · 2:13

    The first record they put out, and everything the band would ever be is already in it. There is no bass player: two guitars, a drum kit, and a hole underneath where the bottom end is supposed to go, which is why it sounds like it is coming out of a wall rather than a stage. Lux Interior is not singing so much as committing to a bit, at length, with no sign that he intends to stop. Play it first and the rest of this rack explains itself.

  • TRACK ONE, SIDE ONE

    TV Set

    Songs the Lord Taught Us · 1980 · 3:12

    The opening track of the first album, and the one that sets the method: take a horror-film premise, deliver it in the flat, pleased voice of somebody describing a hobby, and let the gap between the two do all the work. Nothing is shouted. The guitar keeps going round a figure that never quite lands, the drums are a shoebox, and the whole thing is over before it has explained itself.

  • THE MANIFESTO

    Garbageman

    Songs the Lord Taught Us · 1980 · 3:33

    The nearest thing the band has to a mission statement, and it is an insult aimed squarely at anybody in the audience who has turned up with a checklist of what counts as punk. The riff is a rockabilly figure played with the patience of somebody who intends to keep doing it until you leave or give in. This is the one to hand to a person who thinks a scene gets to decide who is properly in it.

  • THE ONE THAT IS ABOUT YOU

    I Was a Teenage Werewolf

    Songs the Lord Taught Us · 1980 · 3:04

    Slow, dragging, and the only thing here that is genuinely sad. It takes a 1957 drive-in title at its word and plays it as what it obviously always was: a description of being an adolescent who has been informed that they are turning into something, and who would like everybody to stop watching while it happens. Most of us at this foundation have been told a version of that sentence by a professional.

  • THE DIG

    Green Fuz

    Psychedelic Jungle · 1981 · 2:09

    A cover of a 1969 single by the Texas band Green Fuz.

    Two minutes of almost nothing, and it is here to show the working. Half of Psychedelic Jungle is other people's forgotten singles, and this one was a garage record by a band from Texas that essentially nobody had heard. The Cramps were record collectors first and a band second, and their real argument was that the good stuff had already been made and then dropped on the floor. A room about digging things back up owes this one a post.

  • THE ONE THAT CAME BACK

    Goo Goo Muck

    Psychedelic Jungle · 1981 · 3:06

    A cover of a 1962 single by Ronnie Cook and the Gaylads.

    A cover of a 1962 record, buried for nineteen years, dug up by this band in 1981, and then buried again for another forty-one until a dance scene in a television series put it in front of everybody at once. Billboard counted United States on-demand streams going from about 2,500 a week to over 134,000. It is the same song it always was. Nothing about it improved; the room it was being played in changed.

  • THE HIT

    Bikini Girls with Machine Guns

    Stay Sick! · 1989 · 3:18

    As close to a chart record as this band ever came, and it is still a novelty premise played absolutely straight for three minutes. Poison Ivy's guitar sound is the whole argument here: enormous, filthy, and doing something so simple that you can hear exactly how it is done and still not do it. She wrote, arranged and produced most of what this band sounds like, which is worth saying out loud, because the singer is the one who gets remembered.

  • THE ONE THIS ROOM IS NAMED AFTER

    The Creature from the Black Leather Lagoon

    Stay Sick! · 1989 · 3:07

    The song this whole place is named out of. A monster-movie title with a wardrobe joke wedged into the middle of it, which is the band's entire operating principle in one line: take the thing that is supposed to frighten people, put it in better clothes, and let it get on with its evening. There is no moral and nobody is cured at the end. The creature is simply out, and the record is on its side.

  • TWENTY-FIVE YEARS LATER

    Big Black Witchcraft Rock

    Fiends of Dope Island · 2003 · 3:28

    From the last album they made, a quarter of a century after the first single in this rack, and the remarkable thing is how little has been conceded. No orchestra, no ballad, no apologetic acoustic number, nobody has learned to play the bass. Two people found a thing they liked in 1976 and were still doing it, unimproved, in 2003. That is not a failure to develop. That is the plan working.

Where the facts on this page came from

The band’s history here — formed 1976, active until 2009, Lux Interior and Poison Ivy the only constant members, credited as progenitors of psychobilly, the meeting in Sacramento in 1972, the move by way of Akron to New York in 1975, and the June 1978 concert at the state hospital in Napa filmed by Target Video — was read off Wikipedia’s article on the band on 2026-09-22 rather than written from memory. The two covers in the rack, and the origins named on them, come from the article on Psychedelic Jungle, which is also where the figures on Goo Goo Muck’s revival are cited to Billboard. The video ids, runtimes and channels were resolved against YouTube itself, and the release years against MusicBrainz. We have no page of our own about this band, which is unusual for a room here — most of these rooms are built on something stimpunks.org already argues. This one is not, so everything above is this room’s own, and the sources are named so you can go and disagree with them.
