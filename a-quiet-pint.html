A pub under the street

A Quiet Pint

A pint, something from the stove, and a table that fits you. Made like McAnally’s, in Jim Butcher’s Dresden Files.

A pub a few steps down from the street, with a ramp beside the steps as wide as they are. The only light in here is the street’s. It comes in at the top of the walls, through thirteen small windows set at the level of the pavement, and most of it reaches the tables by way of thirteen old mirrors, each hung at its own height and tipped its own way. So it lands in patches, none the same shape and none in a row, a little dimmer and a little green, which is what old mirror glass gives back. Nothing in here is lit warm: the stove is shut, and nobody lights a lamp.

Thirteen of everything, and no two alike: thirteen tables of thirteen heights, thirteen stools along a bar that meanders, thirteen carved columns standing where they stand, and thirteen ceiling fans. There is no television, no games machine and no jukebox, only a player piano. Nobody has to order anything to sit down.

In the pub

From a seat at the south end, at the height of somebody sitting down, which is where most people in a pub are. Drawn from the plan below, so the two cannot disagree.

A table that fits you

The tables are all different sizes and heights, scattered round the floor. That is an access feature as much as a look: when no two tables are the same height, one of them is likelier to be yours. The plan by the door has every one. Tick what you want and the list keeps the tables that have all of it; with scripts off, the whole list is here to read.

Every table at dining height that stands on posts rather than legs has room under it for a wheelchair or your knees: between 28 and 34 inches high, with at least 27 inches underneath (902.3, 306.3). And every table can be reached from the door by a way at least 36 inches wide (403.5.1), round every column and every chair, which the room’s own tool walks on a grid every time it builds this page, and refuses the page if one cannot.

The columns

The columns are wooden, each a different thickness, standing where they stand, and each is carved with a nursery tale. They break the room up, and they never block it: the plan above walks every way between them.

  • The Twelve Dancing Princesses: a row of shoes, every pair worn through.
  • The Enormous Turnip: a turnip as big as a barrel, and a line of everybody pulling at it, down to a mouse.
  • The Golden Goose: a goose, and a line of people stuck to it and to each other.
  • Jack and the Beanstalk: a beanstalk, climbing the whole of the column to the ceiling.
  • Stone Soup: a pot over a fire, with hands round it, each holding something to put in.
  • The Bremen Town Musicians: a donkey, a dog, a cat and a rooster, standing on each other's backs. Tables 1 and 4 are by it.
  • The Princess and the Pea: a stack of mattresses all the way up the column, and one pea under the bottom of them. Table 3 is by it.
  • Goldilocks and the Three Bears: three chairs, three bowls and three beds, no two of them the same size.
  • The Town Mouse and the Country Mouse: two mice at a table, one of them putting on a coat to go home where it is quiet.
  • Rapunzel: a tower, which is the column itself, with a window at the top and hair let down from it.
  • The Fisherman and His Wife: a fish in a rough sea, and a hut on the shore. Tables 8 and 9 are by it.
  • The Three Billy Goats Gruff: three goats crossing a bridge, and something under it. Table 12 is by it.
  • The Elves and the Shoemaker: a workbench at night, and small shoes finished on it by morning. Table 10 is by it.

The piano

A player piano, which plays a roll of punched paper by itself, and the only music in the pub. It plays only when somebody presses a roll, and one roll at a time, and every roll says how long it runs before you press it. Each is a recording of a real roll, kept on its own page on Wikimedia Commons, where it is public domain, and nothing is fetched from there until you press. Every composer on the shelf died more than seventy years ago, so the music is free everywhere, not only in the United States.

At the bar

Nothing on the board has a price, and nobody has to order anything to sit down. Every card says what is in it, which of the nine major food allergens the FDA names it has, and gluten, and whether it has caffeine. Order anything and it comes to the table you are at; look down to see it.

The bar dips to 34 inches for 59 inches of its length, with no stool in front of it, so anybody sitting down can be served there (904.4.1).

When your order is ready

Either is an ordinary way to get your pint, and nobody asks which, or why.

After McAnally’s

This pub is made like McAnally’s, the pub in Jim Butcher’s Dresden Files where the books’ wizards go to drink. In the books a wizard in the room makes machines go wrong, so the pub keeps nothing that can:

That is a pub built round the people who drink in it, rather than one that asks them to stop being who they are at the door, and the rest of the room follows from it. The asymmetry is for them too:

Nothing here is magic, and nobody in here breaks the lights. But a room arranged so that what people bring in with them has somewhere to go, rather than building up, is a room a great many of us would know, and so is walking into one:

Where this pub parts from Mac’s

Three things in the books are barriers in a room otherwise built for the people in it, so this pub does them differently, and says so.

Here there is a ramp beside the steps, as wide as they are, 60 inches, and longer, because 60 inches is a long way down at one in 12: two runs, a landing to turn on, handrails on both sides, and a landing at the door (405, 505). The steps are 9 of them, each 6.7 inches high on an 11-inch tread (504.2).

The ceiling is still low, and the fans turn in the bays between the beams, above the beams’ line, so the lowest thing over the floor is a beam, 82 inches up (307.4). Nothing in here hangs where anybody walks, however tall.

Here you can fetch it, or say so and it is brought to you, and nobody asks why. The choice is on the bar, above the board.

Neutral ground

Nothing here is signed by anybody’s nations, and no accords hold it. The sign over this door says neutral ground, and means it the way our own Third Places page does, where it is the first thing on a list:

The term third place is Ray Oldenburg’s, from his book The Great Good Place, of 1989, and bars are among the first examples Wikipedia gives of one. What our page adds is the radical part, and the radical part is what this room is for.

House rules

  • Neutral ground. Nobody in here has to be anybody’s host or anybody’s guest, and nobody starts anything.
  • You never have to order anything to sit down, and nothing on the board has a price.
  • Every pint says how strong it is before you order it, and the alcohol-free one comes first, in the same glass, at the same size. Nobody buys a round for anybody, and nobody asks what is in your glass.
  • Fetch your order from the bar, or say so and it is brought to you. Nobody asks why.
  • Sit where you like, and as long as you like.
  • Nothing behind the bar has a screen. The piano is a player piano, and it plays only when somebody presses a roll, one roll at a time.
  • Nothing in the pub moves, at any setting, the fans included.
  • Nobody is counted: not the pints, not the rounds, and not you.

What the pub keeps

  • Nothing about you. The table you sit at and what is on it live in this page and are gone when you leave it. The pub’s own scripts store nothing in your browser and send nothing anywhere.
  • Every card says what is in it before you order it, and every pint how strong it is.
  • Nothing plays until you press a roll, and nothing is fetched from Wikimedia Commons until then. Every roll says how long it runs first.

Whose it is

The brief is Ryan Boren’s, of 2026-10-05, and so is the name. The pub it is made like is Jim Butcher’s: McAnally’s, Mac and everything else in The Dresden Files are his, and nothing here is affiliated with him or his publishers. Ryan’s brief pointed at the Dresden Files fan wiki’s page for McAnally’s, and nothing here is drawn from it: the room is built from the books’ own passages, which Ryan quoted from his copies, and every quotation names its book. The tales on the columns are old and nobody’s; what is carved on each, and every word on the board, are ours. The piano’s rolls are recordings on Wikimedia Commons, each public domain on its own page and named on its card. The measurements are the 2010 ADA Standards’, read on the Access Board’s page. The drawings are Claude’s, written as code. Set in Amarante and Brygada 1918. Where each thing was read is in the liner notes.

Give me the answer
